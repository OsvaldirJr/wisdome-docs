## 🧩 Grammar: How new words are made

**1. Blends (palavras-valise)** — partes de duas palavras:
- brunch (breakfast + lunch), smog (smoke + fog), motel (motor + hotel), podcast (iPod + broadcast), staycation (stay + vacation), Brexit (Britain + exit), infodemic (information + epidemic)

**2. Conversão** — a palavra muda de **classe** sem mudar de forma:
- substantivo → verbo: **to google**, **to text**, **to email**, **to friend**, **to adult** (informal), **to gift**
- verbo → substantivo: **an ask** ("a big ask"), **a read** ("a good read"), **a must-have**, **a fail**
- adjetivo → verbo: **to green** (a cidade), **to empty**

**3. Afixos produtivos:**
- **-ify / -ize**: gamify, monetize, weaponize, Uberize
- **-esque / -ian**: Kafkaesque, Orwellian, Dickensian (como na obra de...)
- **-ism / -ist**: ageism, lookism, ableism
- **-ish**: sevenish, greenish, "Are you happy?" "**Ish.**" (informal)
- **-ee / -er**: trainee, influencer
- **hyper- / mega- / post- / micro- / e- / cyber-**: hyperlocal, mega-rich, post-truth, microplastics, e-learning, cybercrime
- **un-** criativo: unfriend, unfollow, unsend

**4. Compostos:** deepfake, doomscrolling, screen time, side hustle, cancel culture

**5. Siglas:**
- **Acronym** (lido como palavra): NASA, laser (light amplification by...), FOMO, YOLO
- **Initialism** (soletrada): FAQ, DIY, BBC

**6. Back-formation** — criar uma palavra "mais simples" a partir de uma mais longa: **edit** (de editor), **babysit** (de babysitter), **donate** (de donation), **burgle** (de burglar)

**7. Empréstimos:** emoji (japonês), tsunami, schadenfreude (alemão), hygge (dinamarquês) — e do português: **samba, caipirinha, favela, capoeira, piranha**.

**Registro:** neologismos informais (ghosting, adulting) funcionam na fala e em textos leves; em textos formais, prefira termos estabelecidos ou explique o neologismo ("so-called 'doomscrolling'").

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Lexicographer:** People often ask me who decides whether a word is "real". The honest answer is: nobody — and everybody. Dictionaries of English don't create words, and they don't approve them. They observe them. Lexicographers track huge collections of real language — books, newspapers, websites, transcripts — looking for words that are being used widely, by many different people, over a sustained period. A word that appears once in a viral post and then disappears won't make it. But if a word keeps appearing in different contexts, across different types of writing, for several years, it's likely to be added. That's how words like "selfie", "emoji" and "podcast" found their way into major dictionaries. This is quite different from languages that have official academies — Portuguese and French, for example, have institutions that publish official vocabularies or spelling rules. English has never had an equivalent. Its dictionaries are descriptive: they tell you how people actually use the language, not how they ought to.

</details>

## 📖 Reading — Is English being ruined? [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M5-L1/reading.m4a)

Complaints about the decline of the English language are almost as old as the language itself. In the eighteenth century, Jonathan Swift lamented the "corruptions" introduced by fashionable young writers and proposed an academy to fix the language once and for all. Two centuries later, critics worried about the influence of American films; more recently, the targets have been text messages, social media and teenagers. Each generation, it seems, is convinced that it is witnessing the end of proper English. Linguists tend to be considerably more relaxed. From their perspective, change is not a sign of decay but a condition of life. Every living language constantly gains new words and loses old ones, shifts its pronunciation and adjusts its grammar. Many words now considered perfectly respectable began as slang or as "errors". "Mob", for example, was once a disreputable abbreviation of the Latin mobile vulgus; "nice" originally meant "foolish"; and the verb "to contact", now indispensable in any office, was widely condemned as vulgar American usage in the early twentieth century. The digital age has undoubtedly accelerated the process. New words can now spread across the globe in days rather than decades, and the boundaries between speech and writing have blurred. Yet research on young people's writing has generally found little evidence that texting damages literacy; some studies even suggest that playful abbreviation requires a sophisticated awareness of how language works. None of this means that every innovation deserves to survive, or that standards no longer matter. Clear, precise language remains essential in law, science and public life, and most speakers are perfectly capable of switching between registers. The real skill, perhaps, is not resisting change but navigating it: knowing when "ghosting" is the perfect word, and when it would be entirely out of place.

## 📝 Writing

Write an essay (300–350 words): "Language change: decline or evolution?" Use examples of neologisms and word formation from English (and, if you wish, Portuguese), and reach a balanced conclusion.

**Model:**

> Language change: decline or evolution?  
>   
> Every few years, a new wave of anxiety sweeps through the media: young people are destroying the language. Their texts are full of abbreviations, their speech full of slang, and words like "ghosting", "adulting" and "doomscrolling" are invading the dictionary. Is this evidence of decline, or simply evolution in action?  
>   
> There is no doubt that English is changing rapidly. Digital technology has accelerated the creation and spread of new words, many of them formed through familiar processes. Blends such as "staycation" and "infodemic" combine existing words; conversion has turned nouns like "google" and "text" into verbs; and productive suffixes allow speakers to coin terms such as "gamify" or "Uberization" almost effortlessly. Far from being chaotic, these innovations follow patterns that have shaped English for centuries.  
>   
> Indeed, history suggests that today's "corruption" is often tomorrow's standard. Words such as "mob" and "bus" were once condemned as vulgar abbreviations, and the use of "contact" as a verb provoked indignation in the early twentieth century. Few people would now consider them anything but ordinary English. The same process is visible in Portuguese, which has absorbed words like "deletar" and "printar", to the dismay of some purists.  
>   
> That said, it would be naive to claim that all change is neutral. Rapidly multiplying jargon can exclude older people or non-specialists, and the informality of online communication can make it harder for some young people to master formal registers when they need them — in job applications, legal documents or academic writing.  
>   
> The solution, however, is not to resist change but to teach flexibility. A speaker who can use "ghosted" with friends and "failed to respond" in a formal complaint is not a victim of linguistic decline but a skilled user of the language.  
>   
> In conclusion, language change is neither a catastrophe nor a cause for unqualified celebration. It is, quite simply, what living languages do. Our task is not to stop it, but to understand it — and to use every register well.
