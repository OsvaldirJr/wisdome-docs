## 🧩 Grammar: Varieties of English

**1. Britânico x americano** (as diferenças mais comuns):
- **Vocabulário:** flat/apartment, lift/elevator, queue/line, holiday/vacation, pavement/sidewalk, autumn/fall, mobile/cell phone, rubbish/trash, chemist/pharmacy, motorway/highway
- **Ortografia:** colour/color, centre/center, organise/organize (ambos aceitos no UK), travelled/traveled, programme/program, defence/defense
- **Gramática:**
  - **have got** (UK) / **have** (US): "Have you got...?" / "Do you have...?"
  - **Present perfect** mais usado no UK: "I've just eaten" (UK) x "I just ate" (US, comum)
  - **Substantivos coletivos:** "The team **are** playing well" (UK, possível) x "The team **is**..." (US)
  - **Preposições:** at the weekend (UK) / on the weekend (US); different from/to (UK) / different from/than (US)
  - **gotten** (US) / got (UK)
- **Pronúncia:** o "r" final (rhotic no US, non-rhotic em RP: car = "kaa"); o "t" entre vogais (US: water ≈ "wárer"); vogais (bath, can't, tomato)

**2. World Englishes** — variedades legítimas com normas próprias:
- **Inglês indiano:** "do the needful", "prepone" (antecipar, oposto de postpone), "revert" (= responder), "cousin-brother"; uso frequente do contínuo ("I am knowing him")
- **Inglês nigeriano:** "to off the light" (desligar), "to on the TV", "well done" como saudação a quem trabalha
- **Singlish (Singapura):** partículas como **lah**, **lor**, **meh**; "Can or not?", "Got or not?"
- **Inglês sul-africano:** "now-now" (já, já), "just now" (daqui a pouco, às vezes!), "robot" (semáforo)
- **Inglês australiano:** arvo (afternoon), brekkie (breakfast), "no worries"
- **Inglês caribenho/crioulos:** sistemas gramaticais próprios (ex.: Jamaican Patois)

**3. Inglês como língua franca (ELF):** em comunicação internacional, **inteligibilidade** importa mais que sotaque "nativo". Pesquisadores como **Jennifer Jenkins** identificaram um "núcleo" de características de pronúncia que mais afetam a compreensão (certas consoantes, vogais longas x curtas, acento nas palavras importantes) — o resto, como o "th", pode variar sem prejudicar a comunicação.

**4. Atitude C2:** reconheça e **respeite** a variação. Escolha uma variedade-base para escrever (UK ou US) e seja **consistente** na ortografia.

| Britânico | Americano |
|---|---|

**Researcher:** The linguist Braj Kachru, who was born in Kashmir and worked in the United States, proposed an influential model of English around the world, often pictured as three concentric circles. The inner circle includes countries where English is the main first language: the UK, the US, Australia and so on. The outer circle includes countries where English has an official or institutional role as a second language, often as a result of colonial history: India, Nigeria, Singapore, the Philippines. And the expanding circle includes countries where English is learned as a foreign language for international communication — Brazil, China, Japan, Germany, and most of the world. The model has been criticized and revised, but its central insight remains powerful: English no longer belongs only to its native speakers. In fact, most interactions in English today probably take place between people for whom it isn't a first language at all. Which raises a fascinating question: who, now, sets the standard?

## 📖 Reading — The myth of the perfect accent [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M5-L2/reading.m4a)

For decades, many language learners have pursued a single, elusive goal: to sound like a native speaker. Textbooks offered recordings in "standard" British or American accents; learners practised minimal pairs late into the night; and those who failed to eliminate every trace of their first language often felt that they had, in some sense, failed. In recent years, this ideal has come under sustained criticism. The first problem is practical. There is no single native accent to imitate. A speaker from Glasgow sounds very different from one from London, Dublin, Texas or Sydney, and none of these accents is inherently more correct than the others. Even the "standard" British accent traditionally taught to learners, Received Pronunciation, is spoken natively by only a small minority of people in the United Kingdom. The second problem concerns purpose. For the vast majority of learners, English is a means of communicating with people from many different countries, most of whom are not native speakers themselves. In these situations, what matters is not whether one sounds British or American but whether one is understood. Research on English as a lingua franca has suggested that certain features — word stress, the distinction between some long and short vowels, and a number of consonant sounds — are far more important for intelligibility than others. A Brazilian speaker who pronounces "think" as "fink" will almost always be understood; one who misplaces the stress in "comfortable" or "development" may cause genuine confusion. None of this means that pronunciation does not matter. Clear pronunciation is a courtesy to one's listeners, and many learners find great satisfaction in mastering the sounds of a new language. But the goal has shifted from imitation to intelligibility, and from shame to confidence. An accent, after all, is not a defect. It is a sign that someone has learned to live in more than one language — which is, by any measure, an achievement.

**Questions:**
1. What goal have many learners pursued?
2. Why is there no single native accent to imitate?
3. What matters most in lingua franca communication?
4. Which features does research suggest are most important for intelligibility?

## 📝 Writing

Write an article (300–350 words) for an international student magazine: "Whose English is it anyway?" Discuss the global spread of English, the varieties that have emerged and what this means for learners like you.

**Model:**

> Whose English is it anyway?  
>   
> When I started learning English as a child in Brazil, I believed there was one "correct" version of the language, spoken by people in London or New York, and that my job was to imitate it as closely as possible. Every trace of my Portuguese accent felt like a mistake. It took me many years — and many conversations with people from very different places — to realize how wrong that idea was.  
>   
> English today is spoken by an estimated one and a half billion people, but only a minority of them learned it as their first language. It is the official or institutional language of countries as diverse as India, Nigeria, Singapore and the Philippines, each of which has developed its own vocabulary, rhythms and expressions. An Indian colleague might ask me to "revert" on an email; a Nigerian friend might ask me to "off the light"; a Singaporean classmate might end a sentence with "lah". These are not errors. They are features of established varieties of English, as legitimate as the British "queue" or the American "sidewalk".  
>   
> For learners, this has liberating implications. First, there is no single accent we are obliged to imitate. What matters in international communication is intelligibility: speaking clearly, stressing the right syllables and adapting to our listeners. Second, we are not merely visitors in someone else's language. As members of the global community of English users, we have a stake in it — and perhaps even a right to shape it.  
>   
> That does not mean anything goes. In formal writing, consistency and accuracy still matter, and learners benefit from understanding the major standard varieties. But the old hierarchy, in which native speakers were the only judges of what was correct, is giving way to something more democratic.  
>   
> So whose English is it? The honest answer is: everyone's who uses it. And that, I believe, is the best reason of all to keep learning it — accent and all.
