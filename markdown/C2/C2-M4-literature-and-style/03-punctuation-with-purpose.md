## 🧩 Grammar: Punctuation for meaning and style

**1. Ponto e vírgula (;)** — liga **duas orações completas** relacionadas, sem conjunção:
- "It was late; everyone had gone home."
- Antes de advérbios conectivos: "The plan was risky; **however**, it worked."
- Em listas complexas com vírgulas internas: "We visited Recife, Pernambuco; Salvador, Bahia; and Natal, Rio Grande do Norte."

**2. Dois-pontos (:)** — **anuncia** o que vem: explicação, lista, citação, conclusão.
- "She had one goal: to win." / "The reason was simple: money."
- ⚠️ O que vem **antes** dos dois-pontos deve ser uma oração completa: ❌ "The ingredients are: eggs, flour..." ✅ "You need three ingredients: eggs, flour and milk."

**3. Travessão (—)** — interrupção, ênfase, reviravolta, aposto dramático:
- "He said yes — eventually."
- "Three people — my mother, my sister and a stranger — saw it happen."
- Mais informal e enfático que parênteses; em textos acadêmicos, use com moderação.

**4. Erros clássicos:**
- **Comma splice** (duas orações completas só com vírgula): ❌ "It was late, everyone had gone home." → use ponto, ponto e vírgula ou conjunção.
- ❌ "The plan was risky, however it worked." → "The plan was risky; however, it worked."
- **Run-on sentence:** duas orações sem nenhuma pontuação.

**5. Vírgula de Oxford** (antes do *and* final de uma lista) — opcional, mas evita ambiguidades:
- "I'd like to thank my parents, Ana**,** and God." (três grupos, não "meus pais, que são Ana e Deus")

**6. Variedade de frases (estilo):**
- **Frase cumulativa** — ideia principal no início, detalhes acumulados depois: "She walked into the sea, the water cold against her legs, the sun low over the horizon, the gulls crying above."
- **Frase periódica** — suspense, ideia principal **no fim**: "After years of struggle, countless rejections and one long, sleepless night, she finally said yes."
- **Fragmento intencional** — impacto: "He never came back. Never."
- **Regra de ouro:** alterne frases longas e curtas. Uma frase curta depois de várias longas **chama atenção**.

| Sinal | Uso | Exemplo |
|---|---|---|
| ; (semicolon) | liga orações completas relacionadas | It rained; we stayed in. |
| ; however, | antes de conectivo | It was hard; however, we finished. |
| : (colon) | anuncia explicação/lista | One problem remained: money. |
| — (dash) | interrupção, ênfase | She agreed — reluctantly. |
| Oxford comma | evita ambiguidade | eggs, ham, and cheese |
| Fragmento | impacto intencional | Silence. Then a scream. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L3/example-01.m4a) The museum was enormous; we saw only a fraction of it in a single day. — _O museu era enorme; vimos apenas uma fração dele num único dia._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L3/example-02.m4a) There was only one explanation for the missing money: someone in the office had taken it. — _Só havia uma explicação para o dinheiro desaparecido: alguém no escritório o tinha pegado._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L3/example-03.m4a) After three hours of debate, two rounds of voting and one dramatic walkout, the committee finally reached a decision — to postpone the decision. — _Depois de três horas de debate, duas rodadas de votação e uma saída dramática, o comitê finalmente chegou a uma decisão — adiar a decisão._

> 💡 **Dica:** O **comma splice** é o erro de pontuação mais comum de brasileiros escrevendo em inglês, porque em português é mais tolerado. Revise seus textos procurando vírgulas que separam duas orações completas — e troque por **ponto, ponto e vírgula ou conjunção**.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ I love Lisbon, it's a beautiful city.  
  ✅ **I love Lisbon; it's a beautiful city. / I love Lisbon. It's... / I love Lisbon because it's...**  
  Comma splice: duas orações completas não se ligam só com vírgula.
- ❌ The results were good, however we need more data.  
  ✅ **The results were good; however, we need more data.**  
  however como conectivo pede ponto e vírgula (ou ponto) antes e vírgula depois.
- ❌ The main problems are: cost and time.  
  ✅ **There are two main problems: cost and time.**  
  Antes dos dois-pontos, use uma oração completa.

<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Columnist:** Few punctuation marks inspire as much passion as the semicolon. Kurt Vonnegut famously advised writers not to use them at all, mocking them as pretentious — before, with typical irony, using one himself a few pages later. Other writers adore them. To their fans, the semicolon is the most elegant mark in the language: stronger than a comma, gentler than a full stop, it joins two ideas that belong together without forcing them into a single sentence. The truth, as usual, lies somewhere in between. Overused, semicolons can make writing feel heavy and academic. Used well, they create subtle connections that no other mark can manage. My advice? Before you use one, ask yourself a simple question: could each side stand alone as a sentence? If not, it's the wrong mark. And if yes — ask whether the two ideas are close enough to share a breath. If they are, go ahead. If not, a full stop will do the job; it usually does.

</details>

## 📖 Reading — The rhythm of prose [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L3/reading.m4a)

Writers have long understood that prose, like music, has rhythm, and that rhythm depends not only on words but on the shape of sentences. In a much-quoted passage from his book The Way We Write, the American writing teacher Gary Provost demonstrated this with a simple experiment. He wrote a paragraph composed entirely of five-word sentences. It was grammatically perfect. It was also, as he pointed out, monotonous. Then he varied the length: a short sentence, a medium one, and then a long, rolling sentence that built and built until it reached its climax. The second version, he argued, did not merely inform; it sang. The lesson is easily overlooked by learners, who often focus on accuracy at the expense of rhythm. A text made entirely of short sentences can sound childish or breathless. A text made entirely of long, complex sentences can exhaust the reader, however impressive each individual sentence may be. The most effective writers alternate. They use long, cumulative sentences to describe and accumulate detail, allowing the reader to settle into a scene; they use periodic sentences, which delay the main point until the end, to build suspense; and they use short sentences — sometimes no more than a word or two — for emphasis, surprise or finality. Punctuation is the instrument that makes this possible. The comma lets a sentence breathe. The semicolon holds two ideas in balance. The colon creates expectation: something important is coming. The dash interrupts, adds, surprises. And the full stop, the most underestimated of all, gives the reader a moment of silence in which a sentence can resonate. None of this can be learned from rules alone. It must be heard. Reading good prose aloud, slowly, remains one of the best ways to develop an ear for rhythm. So does reading your own writing aloud. If you run out of breath, the sentence is too long. If it sounds like a list, it probably needs a variation. Try it.

**Questions:**
1. What did Gary Provost's experiment show?
2. What are cumulative sentences good for?
3. What are periodic sentences good for?
4. What practical advice does the text give?

> The phone call  
>   
> I was nineteen when the phone rang at three in the morning. Nobody calls at three in the morning with good news; I knew that even before I picked it up.  
>   
> It was my uncle. His voice was strange — flat, careful, as if each word had been chosen in advance. My father had been taken to hospital. A heart attack. They didn't know yet.  
>   
> I remember very little of the drive. I remember the empty avenues, the traffic lights changing for nobody, the orange glow of the streetlamps sliding across the windscreen, my mother's hand gripping the door handle so tightly that her knuckles were white. I remember thinking one thing over and over: not yet.  
>   
> The hospital was too bright. Everything in it was too bright: the floors, the walls, the forced smiles of the nurses who told us to wait. So we waited. We waited for four hours; it felt like four days.  
>   
> And then, after a night of silence, after the coffee from the machine and the prayers I didn't know I remembered, after watching every door open and close and open again, a young doctor walked towards us and smiled.  
>   
> He was going to be fine.  
>

>   
> I still think about that drive whenever my phone rings at night. Fear, I learned, is not the opposite of love; it is one of its shapes. And gratitude, when it finally comes, arrives quietly: a doctor's smile, a corridor, a door that opens the right way.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — "Eats, Shoots & Leaves"

O livro britânico *Eats, Shoots & Leaves* (2003), de **Lynne Truss**, virou best-seller improvável — um livro sobre pontuação! O título vem de uma piada: um panda "**eats, shoots and leaves**" (come, atira e vai embora) x "**eats shoots and leaves**" (come brotos e folhas). A vírgula muda tudo. O livro mostra como a pontuação pode ser tema de paixão (e humor) entre falantes de inglês.

- ; liga orações completas relacionadas; antes de however/therefore.
- : anuncia (precisa de oração completa antes).
- — interrompe, enfatiza, surpreende.
- Evite comma splices; varie frases longas, curtas, periódicas e cumulativas.
