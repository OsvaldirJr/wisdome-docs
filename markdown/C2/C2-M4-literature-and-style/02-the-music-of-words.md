## 🧩 Grammar: Sound devices and rhythm

**1. Repetição de sons:**
- **Aliteração** — consoantes iniciais: "**P**eter **P**iper **p**icked...", "**b**ig **b**old **b**rands", "**c**ool, **c**alm and **c**ollected"
- **Assonância** — vogais: "the r**ai**n in Sp**ai**n st**ay**s m**ai**nly...", "h**ea**r the m**e**llow w**e**dding b**e**lls"
- **Consonância** — consoantes no meio/fim: "pi**tt**er-pa**tt**er", "bla**nk** and thi**nk**"
- **Rima** (final ou **interna**): "Once upon a midnight dr**eary**, while I pondered, weak and w**eary**" (Poe, 1845)

**2. Onomatopeia** — palavras que imitam sons: buzz, hiss, whisper, crash, murmur, sizzle, splash, thud, rustle, crackle, tick-tock
- Note que muitas são **verbos** em inglês: "The fire **crackled**." / "Leaves **rustled**."

**3. Ritmo acentual (stress-timed rhythm)**
- O inglês alterna sílabas **fortes** e **fracas**; as fortes caem em intervalos mais ou menos regulares e as fracas são **reduzidas** (schwa /ə/).
- "I **WANT** to **GO** to the **SHOPS**." (3 batidas) — "to the" quase some.
- Isso é muito diferente do português (mais "silábico"), e é uma das razões do sotaque estrangeiro.

**4. Métrica:**
- **Iambo** = fraca + **forte** (da-**DUM**): "be**LIEVE**", "to**DAY**"
- **Pentâmetro iâmbico** = 5 iambos por verso (10 sílabas) — o metro de Shakespeare:
  "Shall **I** com**PARE** thee **TO** a **SUM**mer's **DAY**?"
- **Troqueu** = **forte** + fraca (**DUM**-da): "**DOUB**le, **DOUB**le, **TOIL** and **TROU**ble" (*Macbeth*)

**5. Uso prático:** slogans ("**B**uy **b**etter, **b**uild **b**etter"), títulos ("*Pride and Prejudice*", "*Sense and Sensibility*"), marcas (Coca-Cola, Kit Kat), discursos e escrita criativa.

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L2/example-01.m4a) The fire crackled, the kettle hissed, and somewhere outside the rain began its soft, steady patter. — _O fogo crepitava, a chaleira chiava, e em algum lugar lá fora a chuva começava seu tamborilar suave e constante._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L2/example-02.m4a) Shakespeare wrote most of his plays in iambic pentameter, a rhythm that echoes the natural stress patterns of English speech. — _Shakespeare escreveu a maioria de suas peças em pentâmetro iâmbico, um ritmo que ecoa os padrões naturais de acento da fala inglesa._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L2/example-03.m4a) Good slogans often rely on alliteration and rhythm rather than on logic. — _Bons slogans muitas vezes dependem de aliteração e ritmo, mais do que de lógica._

> 💡 **Dica:** Para melhorar sua **pronúncia** no C2, trabalhe o **ritmo**, não só os sons: leia poesia em voz alta marcando as sílabas fortes e **reduzindo** as fracas. Bater o ritmo com a mão enquanto lê ajuda muito.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ Pronouncing every syllable with equal weight: "I-WANT-TO-GO-TO-THE-SHOPS".  
  ✅ **I WANT to GO to the SHOPS (reduce "to" and "the").**  
  O inglês é stress-timed: sílabas átonas são reduzidas.
- ❌ Confusing alliteration (initial consonants) with rhyme (final sounds).  
  ✅ **Alliteration: big bold brands. Rhyme: cat / hat.**  
  São recursos diferentes.
- ❌ The fire was making a crackle sound.  
  ✅ **The fire crackled.**  
  Onomatopeias costumam funcionar como verbos em inglês.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Ritmo do pentâmetro

Leia batendo levemente na mesa nas sílabas fortes: "shall **I** com**PARE** thee **TO** a **SUM**mer's **DAY**?" — cinco batidas. Depois tente com frases do dia a dia: muitas frases em inglês caem naturalmente nesse ritmo: "I **think** I'll **have** a **cup** of **tea** to**day**."

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Speaker:** Why do children learn nursery rhymes so easily? Why do advertising jingles get stuck in our heads? Sound patterns like rhyme, rhythm and alliteration make language easier to process and to remember — which is why oral traditions all over the world, from ancient epic poetry to Brazilian cordel, relied on them long before writing was common. But there's a more surprising effect. In a study published in 2000, psychologists Matthew McGlone and Jessica Tofighbakhsh presented people with sayings in two versions: one that rhymed, like "Woes unite foes", and one that meant the same thing but didn't rhyme, like "Woes unite enemies". Participants judged the rhyming versions to be more accurate. Researchers sometimes call this the "rhyme-as-reason" effect. The sound of a statement, it seems, can influence whether we believe it. Which is a useful thing to know — both if you're writing a slogan, and if you're trying not to be fooled by one.

## 📖 Reading — Poetry for the ear [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L2/reading.m4a)

For most of human history, poetry was something heard rather than read. Long before printed books, communities preserved their histories, laws and stories in verse, recited from memory by bards, griots and storytellers. Rhythm, rhyme and repetition were not decorations but tools: they made long texts easier to memorize and more pleasurable to hear. The Old English epic Beowulf, for instance, relies on heavy alliteration, each line typically linking two or three stressed words that begin with the same sound. Printing gradually changed the relationship between poetry and sound. As poems came to be read silently on the page, poets began to experiment with visual effects — line breaks, spacing, even the shape of the text. By the twentieth century, many poets had abandoned regular metre and rhyme altogether in favour of free verse, which follows the rhythms of natural speech rather than a fixed pattern. Some critics complained that poetry had lost its music, and with it much of its audience. In recent decades, however, the ear has made a remarkable comeback. Spoken word and slam poetry, performed live and shared online, have attracted a new generation of poets and listeners, many of whom would never have opened a poetry anthology. Hip-hop, with its intricate internal rhymes and rhythmic complexity, has been described by many critics as one of the most significant poetic forms of our time. In Brazil, repentistas improvising in verse, cordel writers and slam competitions in city squares have kept the oral tradition vividly alive. Perhaps the lesson is that poetry has never really stopped being music. Even the quietest poem on a page asks to be heard, if only in the reader's head. To read poetry well — in any language — is to listen as much as to look.

**Questions:**
1. Why were rhythm and rhyme important in oral cultures?
2. What sound feature characterizes Beowulf?
3. How did printing change poetry?
4. What forms have brought "the ear" back to poetry?

## 📝 Writing

Write a short prose poem or descriptive piece (200–260 words) about a place and its sounds (a market, a beach, a city at night, a rainy day). Use at least eight onomatopoeic words and four other sound devices. Then write two lines explaining your choices.

**Model:**

> Rain in Belém, three o'clock  
>   
> It begins, as it always begins, with a whisper.  
>   
> A few fat drops tap on the tin roof of the market: tap, tap, tap, like a polite visitor. The vendors glance up, then down again. They know what's coming.  
>   
> Then the sky splits. The rain rushes in, roaring on the roofs, drumming on the awnings, hammering the river until the whole city seems to hum. Gutters gurgle and overflow. Car tyres hiss through the flooded streets. A bus splashes past, and a woman shrieks, then laughs, her shopping bags soaked.  
>   
> Under the mango trees, the world shrinks to a single sound — a steady, silver static, so loud it swallows every voice. Somewhere a radio crackles, fighting to be heard. A dog whimpers in a doorway. A child stamps in a puddle, delighted, defiant.  
>   
> And then, as suddenly as it started, it stops.  
>   
> Drip. Drip. Drip.  
>   
> The leaves let go of the last of the water. The sun slides out from behind the clouds, and the steam rises softly from the warm, wet stones. The vendors shake their tarpaulins and shout their prices again. Birds begin, cautiously, to sing.  
>   
> In Belém, they say you can set your watch by the rain. Three o'clock, every afternoon: the city's own symphony, played loud and short and true.  
>   
> — Choices: onomatopoeia (tap, roaring, gurgle, hiss, splashes, shrieks, crackles, whimpers, drip) recreates the soundscape; alliteration (steady, silver static; delighted, defiant) and short sentences mimic the rhythm of the storm.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — Slam e cordel no Brasil

O **slam** (competição de poesia falada) chegou ao Brasil nos anos 2000 e hoje acontece em praças e escolas de todo o país, com campeonatos nacionais e participação em competições internacionais. A **literatura de cordel**, por sua vez, foi reconhecida como **patrimônio cultural imaterial brasileiro** pelo IPHAN em 2018. Ambos mostram que a poesia oral, baseada em ritmo e rima, continua viva.
