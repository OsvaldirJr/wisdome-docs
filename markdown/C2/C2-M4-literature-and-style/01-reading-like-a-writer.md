## 🧩 Grammar: Free indirect speech and the historic present

**1. Os três modos de representar pensamento:**
- **Discurso direto:** She thought, "**Why does** he always **do** this? **I'm** tired of it."
- **Discurso indireto:** She wondered **why he always did** that. She **was** tired of it.
- **Discurso indireto livre:** She looked at the clock. **Late again. Why did he always do this? Well, she was tired of it.**

**Características do discurso indireto livre:**
- **Terceira pessoa e passado** (como no indireto): "she was", "did he"
- **Sem verbo introdutório** ("she thought", "she wondered") — a mente da personagem entra direto na narração
- **Marcas da fala/pensamento da personagem:** perguntas, exclamações, interjeições, gírias, frases incompletas, dêiticos como **now, here, today, tomorrow** (em vez de then, there, the next day)
  - "Tomorrow she **would** tell him. **Yes**, tomorrow. **Enough** was enough!"
- **Efeito:** intimidade com a personagem **e** distância irônica ao mesmo tempo — o leitor nunca sabe se o julgamento é do narrador ou da personagem.

**2. O presente histórico** — narrar o passado no presente para criar **imediatismo**:
- Na fala informal: "So **I'm** at the bus stop, and this guy **comes** up to me and **says**..."
- Na ficção: "It **is** 1964. A woman **stands** at the window, waiting."
- Em manchetes e resumos de enredo: "In chapter 3, Capitu **meets** Bentinho..." (convenção para resumir obras!)

**3. Distância narrativa:**
- **Onisciente:** o narrador sabe tudo, entra em várias mentes.
- **Limitado:** segue uma personagem.
- **Não confiável:** o narrador distorce ou ignora a verdade (ex.: Bento Santiago em *Dom Casmurro*).

**4. Tempo verbal ao escrever sobre literatura:** use o **presente** ("literary present"): "Austen **uses** irony to..." / "The narrator **reveals**..."

| Modo | Exemplo |
|---|---|
| Direto | He thought, "I'll never forgive her." |
| Indireto | He thought that he would never forgive her. |
| Indireto livre | He would never forgive her. Never! |
| Presente histórico | So she walks in and says... |
| Literary present | In the novel, Capitu smiles... |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L1/example-01.m4a) Clara closed the letter. So that was it. Twenty years, and he hadn't even bothered to call. Well, she would show him. — _Clara fechou a carta. Então era isso. Vinte anos, e ele nem se dera ao trabalho de ligar. Pois bem, ela ia mostrar a ele._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L1/example-02.m4a) In Dom Casmurro, the narrator never proves that Capitu betrayed him; he simply asks the reader to believe it. — _Em Dom Casmurro, o narrador nunca prova que Capitu o traiu; ele simplesmente pede que o leitor acredite._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L1/example-03.m4a) It is late afternoon in Recife. A boy runs across the bridge, a kite in his hand, and the river shines like a sheet of metal. — _É fim de tarde em Recife. Um menino atravessa a ponte correndo, uma pipa na mão, e o rio brilha como uma chapa de metal._

> 💡 **Dica:** O português também usa discurso indireto livre — Graciliano Ramos em *Vidas Secas* e Clarice Lispector são mestres. Ao ler romances em inglês, observe as frases sem "she thought" que **soam** como pensamento: é ali que mora a técnica.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Ler em voz alta: vozes do texto

Ao ler discurso indireto livre em voz alta, mude **sutilmente** o tom nas frases que pertencem à personagem: mais rápido, mais emotivo, entonação de pergunta ou exclamação. Essa mudança de "voz" ajuda o ouvinte a perceber de quem é o pensamento.

- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L1/pron-01.m4a) (narrator) She closed the letter. | (character) So THAT was it.

<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Professor:** Free indirect speech is so common in modern fiction that we hardly notice it. But it wasn't always there. Scholars often point to Jane Austen, writing in the early nineteenth century, as one of the first novelists in English to use it with real sophistication. In her novels, the narrator's voice slides almost imperceptibly into a character's thoughts — and back out again — so that we see the world through a character's eyes while also sensing the narrator's ironic distance. A century later, modernist writers like Virginia Woolf and James Joyce pushed the technique further, towards what's called stream of consciousness: the attempt to represent the flow of thought itself, with all its jumps, associations and fragments. In Brazilian literature, writers like Graciliano Ramos and Clarice Lispector explored similar territory. What all these writers share is a fascination with the gap between what people say and what they think — and the conviction that fiction is uniquely able to show us both.

## 📖 Reading — Whose voice is it? [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M4-L1/reading.m4a)

"It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." Few opening lines in English literature are as famous as the first sentence of Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, published in 1813. Read quickly, it sounds like a solemn general statement. Read carefully, it is something far more slippery. Is it really a universal truth? Of course not. The sentence is ironic: what it describes is not a law of nature but the belief — or rather the hope — of the families with unmarried daughters who populate the novel. The next sentence confirms the joke: the "truth", we learn, is fixed in the minds of the neighbours, who regard any wealthy newcomer as "the rightful property of some one or other of their daughters". The real subject of the opening, then, is not rich men but the society that schemes to marry them. This ability to say one thing while meaning another — to adopt the voice of a character or a community while quietly mocking it — is at the heart of Austen's art, and it depends heavily on free indirect speech. Throughout the novel, the narrator moves in and out of characters' minds, especially that of the heroine, Elizabeth Bennet. We share her quick judgements and sharp observations, and we only gradually realize, as she does, how mistaken some of them are. The reader experiences prejudice from the inside before recognizing it from the outside. Two centuries later, the technique remains one of the most powerful tools available to novelists. It allows fiction to do something that no other art form can do so easily: to place us simultaneously inside and outside another person's mind. To read like a writer is, in large part, to learn to hear those two voices at once — and to ask, at every turn, whose voice we are really hearing.

**Questions:**
