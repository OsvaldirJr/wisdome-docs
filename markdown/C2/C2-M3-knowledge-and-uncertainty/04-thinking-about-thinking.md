## 🧩 Grammar: Sophisticated comparisons

**1. all the + comparativo (+ because / for)** — intensificação por causa de algo:
- "The victory was **all the sweeter** because nobody expected it."
- "Her criticism made me **all the more** determined."

**2. none the + comparativo** — sem mudança (ver C2-M1-L2):
- "I listened to the explanation but was **none the wiser**." / "He was **none the worse for** the accident."

**3. the + comparativo..., the + comparativo** (com estruturas complexas):
- "**The more** evidence we collect, **the less** certain the answer becomes."
- "**The sooner** we act, **the better**." (sem verbo)

**4. Correção e reformulação:**
- **not so much X as Y** (não tanto X, mas Y): "It's **not so much** a problem **as** a challenge."
- **more (of) a X than a Y**: "He's **more of** a teacher **than** a manager."
- **less X than Y**: "The decision was **less** rational **than** emotional."
- **rather than**: "We need to think **rather than** react."

**5. Ênfase com quantidade:**
- **no less than / no fewer than** (ênfase no número grande): "**No fewer than** 40 studies have confirmed it."
- **no more than** (ênfase no número pequeno): "It took **no more than** five minutes."
- **nothing short of** (nada menos que): "The result was **nothing short of** miraculous."

**6. Igualdade e proporção:**
- **every bit as + adj + as** (tão... quanto, enfático): "She's **every bit as** capable **as** he is."
- **nowhere near / nothing like as + adj + as** (nem de longe tão...): "It's **nowhere near as** easy **as** it looks."
- **twice / three times as likely to**: "People are **twice as likely to** remember a story **as** a statistic."
- **by far / far and away** + superlativo: "**by far the most** common bias"

| Estrutura | Exemplo |
|---|---|
| all the + comp | It was all the more impressive because she was ill. |
| none the + comp | I was none the wiser. |
| the more..., the less... | The more I learn, the less I know. |
| not so much X as Y | Not so much a holiday as an adventure. |
| no fewer / less than | No fewer than 30 people complained. |
| nothing short of | Nothing short of brilliant. |
| every bit as... as | Every bit as good as the original. |
| nowhere near as... as | Nowhere near as simple as it seems. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L4/example-01.m4a) Confirmation bias is all the more dangerous because we rarely notice it in ourselves. — _O viés de confirmação é ainda mais perigoso porque raramente o percebemos em nós mesmos._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L4/example-02.m4a) The problem with the decision was not so much the information available as the way it was interpreted. — _O problema com a decisão não foi tanto a informação disponível, mas a forma como foi interpretada._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L4/example-03.m4a) Experts are nowhere near as immune to cognitive biases as we might expect. — _Especialistas não são nem de longe tão imunes aos vieses cognitivos quanto poderíamos esperar._

> 💡 **Dica:** "**Not so much X as Y**" é perfeita para **corrigir** uma interpretação comum — ótima em redações C2: "The issue is **not so much** technological **as** political." E "**all the more**" vem quase sempre seguido de **because / for / given**.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ It was the more impressive because she was ill.  
  ✅ **It was all the more impressive because she was ill.**  
  A estrutura é all the + comparativo.
- ❌ It's not so much a problem but a challenge.  
  ✅ **It's not so much a problem as a challenge.**  
  not so much X AS Y.
- ❌ No less than 30 people attended. (contáveis, formal)  
  ✅ **No fewer than 30 people attended.**  
  Com contáveis, o formal é no fewer than (no less than é comum na fala).

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Psychologist:** In his best-selling book Thinking, Fast and Slow, the psychologist Daniel Kahneman, who won the Nobel Prize in economics in 2002, popularized a useful way of describing the mind. System 1 is fast, automatic and intuitive: it recognizes a face, reads a simple sentence, or tells you instantly that two plus two is four. System 2 is slow, effortful and deliberate: it's what you use to calculate 17 times 24 or fill in a tax form. Most of the time, System 1 serves us well. The problem is that it's not so much accurate as confident. It jumps to conclusions, relies on rules of thumb and is easily influenced by irrelevant information — a number you saw a minute ago, for example, can affect your estimate of something completely unrelated. That's called anchoring. System 2 can correct these errors, but it's lazy; it takes effort, and we're often happy to accept the first answer that comes to mind. The lesson isn't that intuition is useless. It's that, the higher the stakes, the more it pays to slow down.

## 📖 Reading — The expert's blind spot [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L4/reading.m4a)

It is natural to assume that expertise protects us from error. The more we know about a subject, surely, the better our judgements become. In many domains, this is true: an experienced radiologist is far more likely to spot a tumour than a beginner, and a chess grandmaster sees patterns invisible to a novice. Yet research on expert judgement offers a more complicated picture. In a famous long-term study, the political scientist Philip Tetlock collected thousands of predictions from experts in politics and economics over many years. When he compared their forecasts with what actually happened, the results were sobering. Many experts performed only slightly better than chance, and in some cases no better than simple statistical rules. Strikingly, the most famous experts — those most often quoted in the media — were often among the least accurate. Tetlock drew on an image from the philosopher Isaiah Berlin to explain the difference. "Hedgehogs" know one big thing: they view the world through a single theory and make bold, confident predictions. "Foxes" know many small things: they draw on multiple perspectives, are comfortable with uncertainty and readily change their minds. On average, foxes made more accurate forecasts. Hedgehogs, however, were all the more appealing to audiences precisely because of their confidence. The lesson is not so much that experts are useless as that confidence and accuracy are by no means the same thing. Indeed, the Dunning–Kruger effect — the finding that people with limited knowledge in a field tend to overestimate their competence — suggests that confidence can sometimes be a sign of ignorance rather than mastery. The best thinkers, it seems, are every bit as knowledgeable as the boldest ones, but nowhere near as certain. For those of us who listen to experts, the implication is clear: be wary of anyone who is never in doubt.

**Questions:**

2. What was surprising about the most famous experts?

## 📝 Writing

Write an opinion article (300–350 words): "Should we trust experts?" Discuss the strengths and limits of expert judgement, refer to cognitive biases and reach a nuanced conclusion. Use at least eight sophisticated comparison structures.

**Model:**

> Should we trust experts?  
>   
> In recent years, "trust the experts" has become both a rallying cry and an object of ridicule. For some, experts are our best defence against misinformation; for others, they are an arrogant elite whose predictions are frequently wrong. The truth, I would argue, is not so much somewhere in the middle as more interesting than either position allows.  
>   
> Let us begin with what experts do well. In fields with clear rules and rapid feedback — medicine, engineering, chess — expertise is nothing short of essential. A trained surgeon is not merely somewhat better than an amateur; she is incomparably better. To dismiss such knowledge would be every bit as foolish as refusing to use a map because maps occasionally contain errors.  
>   
> The picture becomes far murkier, however, when experts attempt to predict complex systems such as economies or elections. Here, research suggests, expert forecasts are often nowhere near as accurate as their confident tone implies. Ironically, the more famous the expert, the more likely he or she is to make bold predictions — and bold predictions are, by definition, the ones most likely to fail.  
>   
> Part of the problem lies in the biases that affect us all. Experts are no less vulnerable to confirmation bias than anyone else; indeed, their reputations may make them all the more reluctant to admit mistakes. Overconfidence, too, is less a sign of deep knowledge than of a failure to appreciate uncertainty.  
>   
> So should we trust experts? Yes — but selectively and intelligently. We should trust them far more in areas where their knowledge can be tested than in areas of pure speculation. We should value those who acknowledge uncertainty over those who never express doubt. And we should judge them by their track record rather than their fame.  
>   
> Ultimately, trusting experts is less a matter of faith than of judgement. The question is not whether to listen to them, but how.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — Kahneman e a economia comportamental
