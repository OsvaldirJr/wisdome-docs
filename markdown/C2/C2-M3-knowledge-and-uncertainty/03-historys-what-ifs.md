## 🔥 Warm-up
"E se Pedro Álvares Cabral nunca tivesse chegado ao Brasil?" "E se a internet tivesse sido inventada em 1900?" A **história contrafactual** — imaginar como o mundo seria se algo tivesse acontecido de outro jeito — é um gênero popular de livros e debates. Para isso, o C2 precisa de **todo o repertório hipotético**, incluindo formas literárias como "**would that**" e "**as though nothing had happened**".

- Which moment in history would you most like to change? What would have happened?
- Is it useful to imagine alternative histories, or just entertaining?

## 🧩 Grammar: Complex hypothetical forms

**1. As if / as though + formas irreais** (comparações hipotéticas):
- **Presente irreal** → past simple / were: "He talks **as if** he **owned** the place." (mas não é dono) / "She looks **as though** she **were** about to cry."
- **Passado irreal** → past perfect: "She acted **as though** nothing **had happened**."
- **Comparação real/provável** → tempo normal: "It looks **as if** it**'s going to** rain." (provavelmente vai)
- **It's as if / It's not as if** (= não é como se): "Why are you worried? **It's not as if** you **failed** the exam."

**2. Desejos literários e enfáticos:**
- **Would that** + past / past perfect (literário, arcaico): "**Would that** I **had** more time!" / "**Would that** we **had known**."
- **If only** (B2) + past / past perfect / would
- **Oh, to be / Oh, for** (exclamação poética): "**Oh, to be** young again!" / "**Oh, for** a cup of tea!"

**3. Suppose / Supposing / Say / Imagine** com diferentes tempos:
- Possibilidade real: "**Suppose** the bus **is** late — what then?"
- Hipótese presente: "**Supposing** you **won** the lottery..."
- Contrafactual passado: "**Suppose** the Portuguese **had never reached** Brazil..."
- "**Say** you **had** a million reais — what would you do?"

**4. Subjuntivo fixo em contrafactuais e argumentos:**
- **if need be** (se for preciso) · **be it X or Y** (seja X ou Y) · **come what may** · **so be it** · **far be it from me to** (longe de mim...) · **as it were** (por assim dizer)
- "**Far be it from me to** criticize, but..." (ironia: vou criticar)

**5. Contrafactuais mistos e cadeias (revisão B2 + extensão):**
- "Had the Portuguese crown **not** moved to Rio in 1808, Brazil **might well have** split into several countries — and we **would** perhaps **be speaking** of 'the Brazils' today."

| Estrutura | Exemplo |
|---|---|
| as if + past (presente irreal) | He acts as if he were the boss. |
| as though + past perfect | She spoke as though she had been there. |
| It's not as if... | It's not as if we have a choice. |
| Would that + past perfect | Would that I had known. |
| Suppose + past perfect | Suppose Napoleon had won. |
| Subjuntivo fixo | if need be / be it... / far be it from me |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L3/example-01.m4a) The minister spoke about the crisis as though it had been entirely unforeseeable. — _O ministro falou da crise como se ela tivesse sido completamente imprevisível._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L3/example-02.m4a) Suppose the Portuguese royal family had stayed in Lisbon in 1807 — would Brazil have become independent in the same way? — _Suponha que a família real portuguesa tivesse ficado em Lisboa em 1807 — o Brasil teria se tornado independente da mesma forma?_
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L3/example-03.m4a) Far be it from me to tell historians how to do their job, but counterfactuals can be illuminating. — _Longe de mim dizer aos historiadores como fazer seu trabalho, mas os contrafactuais podem ser esclarecedores._

> 💡 **Dica:** "**Como se**" em português pede subjuntivo ("como se **fosse**", "como se **tivesse** acontecido"). Em inglês, use **as if / as though** + **past** (= fosse) ou **past perfect** (= tivesse + particípio). E "**It's not as if**" é ótimo para argumentar: "**It's not as if** we didn't warn them!"

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ She behaved as if nothing happened. (o fato aconteceu antes)  
  ✅ **She behaved as if nothing had happened.**  
  Para o passado irreal anterior, use past perfect.
- ❌ Would that I have more time!  
  ✅ **Would that I had more time!**  
  would that + past (presente) ou past perfect (passado).
- ❌ Far be it for me to criticize.  
  ✅ **Far be it from me to criticize.**  
  A expressão fixa é far be it FROM me.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Tom de especulação

Em contrafactuais, falantes usam um ritmo **mais lento e reflexivo**, com entonação ascendente na condição e descendente na consequência: "Suppose the war had never happened ↗... | would we be here today ↘?" "**Far be it from ME** to..." tem tom irônico, com ênfase em ME.

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Professor:** For a long time, many professional historians dismissed counterfactual history as a parlour game. The British historian E. H. Carr famously mocked it, and plenty of scholars still regard it with suspicion. Yet, in a sense, all historical explanation is counterfactual. To say that an event caused another is to imply that, had the first not happened, the second would not have happened either. Suppose a historian claims that a drought caused a revolution. That claim only makes sense if we can imagine the same society without the drought — and ask whether the revolution would still have occurred. Used carefully, counterfactuals help us test explanations and resist the illusion that the past was inevitable. Used carelessly, however, they become fantasy, with each imaginary step piled on another as though we could know the consequences decades later. The trick, most historians who use them would say, is to stay close to the moment: change one thing, ask what was realistically possible, and stop before you start writing a novel.

</details>

## 📖 Reading — The illusion of inevitability [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L3/reading.m4a)

Looking back, it is tempting to see history as a path leading inexorably towards the present. Empires rise and fall as though following a script; revolutions break out because they "had to"; technologies appear exactly when the world is ready for them. Psychologists have a name for part of this tendency: hindsight bias, the inclination, once we know an outcome, to believe that it was predictable all along. In a series of classic experiments in the 1970s, the psychologist Baruch Fischhoff found that people who were told the outcome of a historical event rated that outcome as considerably more likely than people who were not told — and frequently believed they would have predicted it themselves. Counterfactual thinking is one of the most effective antidotes to this illusion. By asking "what if?", we are forced to reconstruct the uncertainty that people at the time actually faced. Suppose an assassination attempt had failed, a ship had sailed a day earlier, or a vote had gone the other way by a handful of ballots. Would the world look the same? Very often, the honest answer is that we cannot be sure — and that uncertainty is itself a valuable insight. It reminds us that the people of the past were not characters walking towards a known ending, but individuals making difficult choices with incomplete information, much as we do. There is a moral dimension to this, too. If the past was inevitable, then nobody was truly responsible for it: things simply happened as they had to. If, on the other hand, outcomes hinged on decisions that could have been different, then those decisions — and the people who made them — can be judged. Far from being idle speculation, then, the question "what if?" may be one of the most serious questions historians, and citizens, can ask. Would that it were asked more often.

**Questions:**
1. What is hindsight bias?
2. What did Fischhoff's experiments find?
3. How does counterfactual thinking help?
4. What is the moral dimension of the argument?

## 📝 Writing

Write a short counterfactual essay (300–350 words): "What if... ?" Choose a historical turning point, change one decision or event, and explore the realistic consequences. Use at least eight complex hypothetical structures and reflect on what the exercise teaches us.

**Model:**

> What if Brasília had never been built?  
>   
> In 1956, Juscelino Kubitschek became president of Brazil with an extraordinary promise: to build a new capital in the empty centre of the country within a single term. Brasília was inaugurated in 1960. Today, it is so familiar that we speak of it as though it had always been inevitable. It was not.  
>   
> Suppose Kubitschek had abandoned the project, as many of his critics demanded. The capital would, in all likelihood, have remained in Rio de Janeiro. Rio would have kept not only its political power but also the jobs, investment and prestige that came with it — and the city's later decline, which many historians partly attribute to the loss of its capital status, might well have been less severe.  
>   
> The effects on the interior would arguably have been even greater. Brasília was intended, as it were, as a heart that would pump life into the centre of the country. Had it never been built, the roads connecting the Centre-West to the rest of Brazil might have taken decades longer to appear, and the development of the Cerrado — for better and for worse — could have followed a very different path.  
>   
> Of course, it is not as if the interior would have remained empty forever. Agricultural expansion was already under way, and some form of integration would doubtless have happened sooner or later. Nor would Brazil necessarily have been better off. Brasília was enormously expensive, and many economists link its cost to the inflation that troubled the country in the following years.  
>   
> Far be it from me to claim certainty about any of this. Counterfactuals cannot be tested; they can only be argued. Yet the exercise is valuable precisely because it reminds us that the Brazil we know hinged on a series of human decisions — bold, controversial and, at the time, deeply uncertain. Would that we remembered this when we assume that today's choices do not matter.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — Ucronia: a história que não aconteceu

A ficção de **história alternativa** é um gênero popular em inglês: *The Man in the High Castle* (Philip K. Dick), *Fatherland* (Robert Harris) e *The Plot Against America* (Philip Roth) imaginam mundos em que a história tomou outro rumo. Em português, o gênero é chamado de **ucronia**. Contrafactuais também são comuns em podcasts e livros de divulgação histórica.
