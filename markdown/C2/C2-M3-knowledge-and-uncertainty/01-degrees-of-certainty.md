## 🔥 Warm-up
Um meteorologista diz "**70% de chance de chuva**". O que isso significa? Cientistas, médicos e analistas precisam comunicar **graus de certeza** com precisão — e o público frequentemente entende errado. No C2, você vai além de "may" e "must": "**In all likelihood**...", "**There's every reason to believe**...", "It **needn't necessarily** mean...".

- How certain do you need to be before you make an important decision?
- Do you trust weather forecasts? Economic forecasts? Why?

## 🧩 Grammar: Fine-grained epistemic modality

**1. Escala de certeza com expressões nominais e adverbiais** (do mais certo ao menos):
- **certo:** it's a foregone conclusion · undoubtedly · without a doubt · there's no question that
- **quase certo:** in all likelihood · in all probability · there's every chance / reason to believe · the odds are (that)
- **provável:** it's (highly) likely that · S is likely to · chances are (that)
- **possível:** conceivably · it's not inconceivable that (litotes!) · there's a (slim / outside) chance that
- **improvável:** it's doubtful whether · S is unlikely to · it's a long shot
- **impossível:** it's inconceivable / unthinkable that · there's no way that (informal)

**2. Modais + advérbios** (graduação fina):
- **may / might / could well** (bem provável) x **may / might just** (pequena chance)
- **can't possibly / couldn't possibly** (impossível) — "She **can't possibly have** known."
- **needn't necessarily** (não implica obrigatoriamente) — "A fall in sales **needn't necessarily** mean a crisis."
- **must surely / must have surely** (dedução forte, com ênfase)
- **would probably / would almost certainly**

**3. It + adjetivo + that / whether:**
- "**It is doubtful whether** the plan will work."
- "**It is highly improbable that**..." / "**It is by no means certain that**..."
- "**It is not inconceivable that**..." (= é possível — mais cauteloso)

**4. Atenção ao "no passado":**
- **must have / can't have / might have + particípio** (dedução)
- "**In all likelihood**, the letter **was** lost in the post."
- "There**'s every chance** he **has** already **left**."

**5. Comunicar incerteza:** separe **o que se sabe**, **o que é provável** e **o que é desconhecido**. Evite falsa precisão ("exactly 73.4%") e falsa certeza ("science proves").

| Grau | Expressões |
|---|---|
| Certo | a foregone conclusion, undoubtedly |
| Quase certo | in all likelihood, there's every chance, the odds are |
| Provável | it's highly likely, chances are |
| Possível | conceivably, it's not inconceivable, may well |
| Improvável | it's doubtful whether, a long shot, might just |
| Impossível | inconceivable, can't possibly |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L1/example-01.m4a) In all likelihood, the virus was circulating for several weeks before the first case was identified. — _Muito provavelmente, o vírus estava circulando por várias semanas antes de o primeiro caso ser identificado._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L1/example-02.m4a) A single cold winter needn't necessarily contradict the evidence for global warming. — _Um único inverno frio não contradiz necessariamente as evidências do aquecimento global._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L1/example-03.m4a) It is doubtful whether the new treatment will be available before the end of the decade. — _É duvidoso que o novo tratamento esteja disponível antes do fim da década._

> 💡 **Dica:** "**Needn't necessarily**" é muito útil para **evitar conclusões precipitadas**: "Correlation needn't necessarily imply causation." E "**It's not inconceivable**" (litotes) soa mais cauteloso que "it's possible".

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ In all likelihoods, it will rain.  
  ✅ **In all likelihood, it will rain.**  
  Expressão fixa no singular.
- ❌ It is doubtful that if the plan will work.  
  ✅ **It is doubtful whether / that the plan will work.**  
  doubtful + whether ou that, sem if duplo.
- ❌ She can't possibly knew.  
  ✅ **She can't possibly have known.**  
  Modal + have + particípio para o passado.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Ênfase na certeza

O grau de certeza também está na voz. Em **can't POSSibly** e **needn't NEcessarily**, o advérbio é **muito acentuado**. Em expressões de incerteza, a entonação tende a ser **mais lenta e suspensa**: "It's... conceivable ↗, I suppose."

- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L1/pron-01.m4a) She can't POSSibly have known.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L1/pron-02.m4a) That needn't NEcessarily be bad.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L1/pron-03.m4a) In ALL LIKElihood, it'll be fine.

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Speaker:** In the 1960s, an analyst at the CIA, Sherman Kent, noticed a worrying problem. Intelligence reports used words like "probable", "likely" and "a serious possibility", but when he asked colleagues what probability these words represented, their answers varied enormously. For some, "a serious possibility" meant a 20 percent chance; for others, 80. Later surveys of the general public have found the same pattern. The word "likely" might mean 60 percent to one person and 90 to another. This matters. A doctor who says a treatment will "probably" work, or a climate report that describes an outcome as "very likely", may be understood very differently by different people. That's why some organizations — the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, for instance — attach specific numerical ranges to their words: "very likely", in their reports, means 90 to 100 percent. The lesson for all of us? When it really matters, it's worth adding a number.

## 📖 Reading — The science of saying "we don't know" [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M3-L1/reading.m4a)

In the early months of the COVID-19 pandemic, scientists faced a communication challenge of unprecedented scale. They were being asked, daily and in front of enormous audiences, to answer questions that could not yet be answered with confidence. How easily does the virus spread? How deadly is it? Do masks help? The honest answer to many of these questions was, at first, "We don't know — but here is what we think is likely." That answer proved remarkably difficult to communicate. Audiences, understandably, wanted certainty. Politicians often preferred clear messages. And when advice changed as evidence accumulated — as it inevitably did — many people concluded that the experts had been wrong, or worse, dishonest. Yet changing one's mind in response to new evidence is not a failure of science; it is the very essence of it. Researchers who study risk communication have drawn several lessons from the experience. First, uncertainty should be acknowledged openly and early. Studies suggest that admitting uncertainty does not necessarily damage trust; in some cases, audiences find it more credible than false confidence. Second, it helps to distinguish clearly between what is known, what is probable and what is still unknown. Third, communicators should explain in advance that advice may change, and why. If people expect revisions, they are less likely to interpret them as contradictions. None of this is easy. A message hedged with qualifications is, in all likelihood, less memorable than a confident slogan. But there is every reason to believe that, over time, honesty about uncertainty builds a deeper and more durable kind of trust — one that can survive the inevitable moments when the evidence shifts.

**Questions:**
1. What was the honest answer to many early pandemic questions?
2. Why did changing advice cause problems?
3. Does admitting uncertainty damage trust, according to studies?
4. Name two lessons from risk communication research.

## 📝 Writing

Write a short article (280–320 words) for a science magazine explaining a scientific or economic question that is still uncertain (e.g. the future of AI, the Amazon tipping point, a new medical treatment). Distinguish clearly between what is known, likely and unknown, using at least eight precise expressions of certainty.

**Model:**

> Will we ever talk to animals?  
>   
> In recent years, a number of research projects have begun using artificial intelligence to analyse the sounds of animals — from sperm whales to bats to crows — in the hope of decoding their communication. Headlines have promised that we may soon be able to "talk to" other species. How realistic is that?  
>   
> What we know is this: many animals use complex vocal systems. Some species of birds and whales produce sequences of sounds that follow regular patterns, and researchers have shown that certain calls are associated with particular situations, such as warnings of specific predators. It is beyond doubt that animals communicate, and in some cases in surprisingly sophisticated ways.  
>   
> What is likely is that AI will help scientists detect patterns that humans would never notice. Machine learning is remarkably good at finding structure in large amounts of data, and there is every reason to believe that it will reveal more about how and when animals use particular sounds. In all likelihood, we will soon have much more detailed "dictionaries" of animal calls.  
>   
> What remains unknown — and is, frankly, doubtful — is whether any animal communication system is a language in the human sense, with grammar and the ability to express new ideas. Finding patterns needn't necessarily mean finding meaning. A model might predict which sound a whale will make next without understanding what, if anything, the sound refers to.  
>   
> As for a two-way conversation, that remains a long shot. Even if we could produce the right sounds, it is by no means certain that animals would interpret them as we intend. It is not inconceivable that we will one day exchange simple signals with another species. But the odds are that the first breakthroughs will be smaller, stranger and more interesting than the headlines suggest.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — "Words of estimative probability"

O ensaio **"Words of Estimative Probability"** (1964), de Sherman Kent, é um clássico sobre como palavras de probabilidade são mal interpretadas. Hoje, organizações como o **IPCC** (clima) e algumas agências de inteligência usam **escalas oficiais** que ligam palavras a porcentagens — um bom exemplo de como a precisão linguística pode ter consequências globais.
