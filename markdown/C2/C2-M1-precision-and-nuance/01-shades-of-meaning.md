## 🧩 Grammar: Near-synonyms: connotation, collocation and register

No C2, quase-sinônimos raramente são intercambiáveis. Eles se diferenciam em **quatro dimensões**:

**1. Conotação (juízo de valor)**
- positivo → neutro → negativo: **slender** → **thin** → **skinny** → **scrawny**
- **determined** (+) / **persistent** (=) / **stubborn** (−) / **obstinate** (− formal)
- **frugal / thrifty** (+) / **careful with money** (=) / **mean / stingy / tight-fisted** (−)
- **confident** (+) / **self-assured** (=) / **arrogant / cocky** (−)

**2. Collocation (com o que a palavra combina)**
- **commit** a crime / an error ✔ · **make** a mistake ✔ · ❌ commit a mistake
- **heavy** rain / **strong** wind / **high** temperature
- **utterly** combina com palavras negativas ou extremas: utterly **ridiculous / exhausted** (raro: utterly delighted)
- **broad** daylight, **bitterly** cold, **acutely** aware, **painfully** shy, **blissfully** unaware

**3. Registro**
- **commence** (formal) / **start** (neutro) / **kick off** (informal)
- **deceased** (formal/jurídico) / **dead** (neutro) / **passed away** (eufemismo)
- **inebriated** (formal/humorístico) / **drunk** (neutro) / **hammered** (gíria)

**4. Intensidade**
- **like** < **be fond of** < **adore** < **worship**
- **annoyed** < **irritated** < **angry** < **furious** < **livid**
- **interesting** < **fascinating** < **riveting**

**Uso estratégico:** a escolha de palavras revela **posição**. Jornalistas, políticos e advogados escolhem com cuidado: "**reform**" (+) x "**cuts**" (−); "**protesters**" x "**rioters**"; "**estate tax**" x "**death tax**".

| Positivo | Neutro | Negativo |
|---|---|---|
| determined | persistent | stubborn / pig-headed |
| frugal / thrifty | careful with money | stingy / tight-fisted |
| slender / slim | thin | skinny / scrawny / gaunt |
| confident | self-assured | arrogant / cocky |
| curious / inquisitive | interested | nosy / prying |
| assertive | direct | pushy / aggressive |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L1/example-01.m4a) What her supporters call principled, her critics call obstinate. — _O que seus apoiadores chamam de íntegro, seus críticos chamam de obstinado._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L1/example-02.m4a) He was blissfully unaware that the whole office had been talking about him. — _Ele estava felizmente alheio ao fato de que o escritório inteiro andava falando dele._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L1/example-03.m4a) The government described the measures as "reforms"; the unions called them "cuts". — _O governo descreveu as medidas como "reformas"; os sindicatos as chamaram de "cortes"._

> 💡 **Dica:** Use um **dicionário de collocations** (como o *Oxford Collocations Dictionary*) e um **corpus** (buscadores de frases reais) para checar combinações. No C2, a pergunta não é "essa palavra existe?", mas "**um nativo diria assim?**"

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ She's very skinny and elegant.  
  ✅ **She's very slender and elegant.**  
  skinny tem conotação negativa; slender combina com elegância.
- ❌ He committed a mistake.  
  ✅ **He made a mistake. / He committed an error (formal).**  
  commit combina com crime, error, offence — não com mistake.
- ❌ I was utterly pleased with the result.  
  ✅ **I was thoroughly / immensely pleased with the result.**  
  utterly costuma combinar com palavras negativas ou absolutas.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Atitude na voz

A conotação também está no **tom**. A mesma palavra neutra pode ficar negativa com ênfase e entonação: "He's very... *confident*" (com pausa e tom descendente = arrogante). No C2, perceba e use essa camada.

- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L1/pron-01.m4a) He's very... CONfident. (= arrogante)
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L1/pron-02.m4a) She's... DEtermined, let's say. (= teimosa)
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L1/pron-03.m4a) It's... unUSual. (= estranho)

## 🎧 Listening — Same facts, different words

_Uma professora de linguística mostra como a escolha de palavras muda a percepção de um mesmo fato._

**Listen first, without reading.** [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L1/listening-full.m4a)

**Questions:**
1. What did participants in the 1974 experiment watch?
2. What was the only difference between the groups?
3. What happened a week later?
4. What does the speaker mean by "a small argument in disguise"?

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Speaker:** In a classic experiment published in 1974, the psychologists Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed participants films of car accidents and then asked them how fast the cars were going. The only difference between the groups was a single verb. Some were asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed into" each other; others heard "collided", "bumped", "hit" or "contacted". Those who heard "smashed" gave the highest speed estimates. A week later, they were also more likely to report having seen broken glass — even though there was none in the film. One word changed not just how people judged the event, but what they remembered. Journalists, lawyers and politicians understand this intuitively. Is a sudden rise in prices a "spike" or a "correction"? Are people who cross a border "migrants", "refugees" or "illegals"? Each choice is a small argument in disguise. And at the highest level of language, noticing those choices — and making them consciously — is perhaps the most important skill of all.

</details>

## 📖 Reading — The tyranny of the thesaurus [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L1/reading.m4a)

Every writing teacher has encountered it: the essay in which every second word appears to have been lifted from a thesaurus. "The protagonist perambulated pensively through the verdant arboretum", writes the student, when what he means is that the main character walked through the park, thinking. The impulse is understandable. Learners — and, for that matter, many native speakers — are often taught that variety is a virtue and that repetition is a sin. A thesaurus seems to offer an endless supply of alternatives. The difficulty is that a thesaurus lists words that are similar, not words that are interchangeable. "Slender", "skinny", "gaunt" and "emaciated" all denote thinness, but they evoke four very different people. "Notorious" and "famous" both describe someone widely known, yet only one would be welcome on a reference letter. Even apparently neutral synonyms differ in collocation and register: one "commences" a legal procedure but rarely "commences" a holiday. Linguists sometimes claim that there are no true synonyms in any language — that if two words were genuinely identical in every context, one of them would eventually fall out of use or acquire a distinct meaning. Whether or not this is strictly true, it captures something important. Words carry histories, associations and social signals that no list can fully record. The solution is not to abandon the thesaurus but to treat it as a starting point rather than an answer. Before adopting an unfamiliar word, a careful writer checks how it is actually used: in which contexts, with which neighbours, in what tone. Precision, not variety, is the hallmark of good writing — and, at the highest levels of proficiency, of good speaking too. As the novelist Elmore Leonard memorably advised: if it sounds like writing, rewrite it.

**Questions:**
1. What is the problem with the student's sentence?
2. What does a thesaurus list, according to the text?
3. What do some linguists claim about synonyms?
4. What is the hallmark of good writing, according to the writer?

## 📝 Writing

Write a reference letter (280–320 words) for a former colleague with both strengths and weaknesses. Be honest, but choose your words so that every statement is accurate and professional. Then write two lines explaining three of your word choices.

**Model:**

> Reference for Mr. André Santos  
>   
> To whom it may concern,  
>   
> I am pleased to provide a reference for André Santos, who worked as a Senior Analyst in my team at Horizonte Consultoria from March 2021 to June 2025.  
>   
> André is a highly capable analyst with an exceptional eye for detail. His reports were consistently thorough and meticulously researched, and on several occasions he identified errors in financial models that others had overlooked. Clients frequently commented on the clarity of his written work.  
>   
> He is also a determined professional. When a project encountered difficulties, he was invariably the last person to give up, and he often worked well beyond what was expected of him to meet a deadline. This persistence was particularly valuable during a complex restructuring project in 2023, which he helped to bring to a successful conclusion.  
>   
> André holds strong views on how work should be done and expresses them directly. While this occasionally led to lively discussions within the team, his contributions were always motivated by a genuine commitment to quality. He would, I believe, thrive in a role that allows him a degree of independence and values precision above speed.  
>   
> I have no hesitation in recommending him for a position that requires analytical rigour and dedication. Should you require any further information, please do not hesitate to contact me.  
>   
> Yours faithfully,  
>   
> Mariana Duarte  
> Head of Analysis, Horizonte Consultoria  
>   
> —  
> Word choices: "determined" rather than "stubborn" emphasizes the positive side of his persistence; "expresses them directly" is a neutral way of acknowledging that he could be blunt; "lively discussions" signals conflict without exaggerating it.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — "Damning with faint praise"

Em inglês, **damning with faint praise** (condenar com elogio fraco) é a arte de criticar elogiando pouco. Numa carta de referência britânica, frases como "He was always **punctual**" ou "She **did her best**" podem ser lidas como **sinais negativos** — porque o autor não encontrou nada melhor para dizer. O que **não** se diz também comunica!
