## 🧩 Grammar: Binomials, proverbs and creative manipulation

**1. Binômios (binomials)** — pares de palavras ligados por **and / or / but**, com **ordem fixa**:
- **Sinônimos/reforço:** safe and sound, peace and quiet, pick and choose, first and foremost
- **Opostos:** pros and cons, ups and downs, give and take, sooner or later, black and white, back and forth
- **Repetição/rima:** neck and neck (empatados), touch and go, wear and tear, high and dry, rough and ready
- **Preposições:** by and large, on and off, over and above, out and about
⚠️ A ordem **não muda**: ❌ "sound and safe", ❌ "cons and pros".

**Trinômios:** lock, stock and barrel (tudo); hook, line and sinker (completamente); tall, dark and handsome.

**2. Provérbios** — nativos frequentemente dizem **só a primeira metade**:
- "When in Rome..." (do as the Romans do)
- "Speak of the devil..." (and he shall appear) — quando alguém de quem se falava aparece
- "Too many cooks..." (spoil the broth)
- "The early bird..." (catches the worm)
- "Every cloud..." (has a silver lining)
- "Don't count your chickens..." (before they hatch)

**3. Manipulação criativa** — manchetes, publicidade e humor alteram expressões fixas, e o leitor C2 precisa reconhecer o original:
- "**Every cloud has a silver lining**" → manchete sobre computação em nuvem: "**Every cloud has a cyber lining**"
- "**Better late than never**" → "**Better late than pregnant**" (humor)
- "**All's well that ends well**" → "All's well that **sends** well" (anúncio de correio)

**4. Uso adequado** — idioms em excesso soam **artificiais** ("It's raining cats and dogs, so let's kill two birds with one stone..."). Use-os **com moderação** e no **registro certo**: "by and large" e "first and foremost" funcionam até em textos formais; "lock, stock and barrel" é mais informal/jornalístico.

| Tipo | Exemplos |
|---|---|
| Sinônimos | safe and sound, peace and quiet, first and foremost |
| Opostos | pros and cons, ups and downs, give and take |
| Rima/aliteração | wear and tear, touch and go, part and parcel |
| Preposições | by and large, on and off, out and about |
| Trinômios | lock, stock and barrel; hook, line and sinker |
| Provérbios cortados | When in Rome... / Speak of the devil... |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L3/example-01.m4a) By and large, critics have welcomed the reform, though there have been a few dissenting voices. — _De modo geral, os críticos receberam bem a reforma, embora tenha havido algumas vozes discordantes._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L3/example-02.m4a) Long hours and constant travel are part and parcel of being a foreign correspondent. — _Longas jornadas e viagens constantes fazem parte da vida de correspondente internacional._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L3/example-03.m4a) He fell for the scam hook, line and sinker. — _Ele caiu no golpe direitinho._

> 💡 **Dica:** Para o **resto do provérbio**, procure no dicionário pela palavra principal ("Rome", "devil", "cooks"). E evite traduzir provérbios brasileiros ao pé da letra: "**Quem não tem cão caça com gato**" → algo como "**Needs must**" ou "**Make do with what you have**".

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: O "and" reduzido

Em binômios, o **and** quase desaparece: "safe'n'sound", "pros'n'cons", "rock'n'roll". As duas palavras principais recebem acento e o par soa como **uma unidade só**: "PART'n'PARcel", "TOUCH'n'GO".

## 🎧 Listening — Why can't we say "pepper and salt"?

_Uma linguista explica por que binômios têm ordem fixa._

**Listen first, without reading.** [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L3/listening-full.m4a)

**Questions:**
1. Which word tends to come first in a binomial?
2. What vowel pattern explains "zig-zag" and "tick-tock"?
3. What does "husband and wife" show?
4. Why should learners learn binomials as units?

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Linguist:** Why do English speakers say "salt and pepper" but never "pepper and salt"? Or "rock and roll", "black and white", "ladies and gentlemen"? Linguists have identified several tendencies — not strict rules, but strong patterns. First, the shorter word tends to come first: "salt and pepper", "bread and butter". Second, when vowels differ, words with higher, front vowels — like the "i" in "zig" — tend to precede lower, back vowels, like the "a" in "zag". That's why it's "zig-zag", "tick-tock", "flip-flop" and never the other way round. Third, cultural or logical priority often matters: "cause and effect", "before and after", "husband and wife" — though the last example also shows how these orders can reflect social hierarchies of the past. Interestingly, Portuguese often shows similar patterns. But when the orders differ between languages, learners tend to transfer their own — which is why, at C2, these small, fixed pairs are worth learning as single units.

</details>

## 📖 Reading — The headline writer's toolkit [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M1-L3/reading.m4a)

Few writers handle fixed expressions with as much mischief as the authors of newspaper headlines. Limited to a handful of words and competing for the reader's attention, headline writers — sub-editors, in British newsrooms — have long relied on a simple trick: take a familiar phrase and twist it. A story about a theft from a cheese factory becomes "Grate Expectations"; an article on the decline of hand-written letters is headed "Signed, Sealed, Deleted"; a report on rising house prices in a seaside town appears as "Home and Dry? Not Any More". The technique depends entirely on recognition. The reader must instantly recall the original — Dickens's Great Expectations, the song "Signed, Sealed, Delivered", the idiom "home and dry" — and register the change. The pleasure lies in the gap between the expected and the actual, and in the small flash of understanding that bridges it. Advertising uses the same mechanism, as do comedians, politicians and, increasingly, social media users. For language learners, this presents a particular challenge. It is perfectly possible to understand every word of a headline and still miss its point entirely. A learner unfamiliar with "Every cloud has a silver lining" will find nothing remarkable in a technology column titled "Every Cloud Has a Cyber Lining"; the native reader, by contrast, sees a joke, an argument and a cultural reference compressed into six words. This is why fixed expressions matter far more at the highest levels than their apparent triviality might suggest. They are part and parcel of a shared cultural memory — the background against which a great deal of creative language is written. To read like an insider, one must first know what the insiders know by heart. Only then can one appreciate, and eventually produce, the twist.

**Questions:**
1. What trick do headline writers use?
2. What does the technique depend on?
3. Why can learners miss the point of a headline?
4. Why are fixed expressions important at the highest levels?

## 📝 Writing

Write a light-hearted magazine column (280–320 words) about a topic of your choice (moving house, working from home, family holidays, being a parent). Use at least eight binomials or fixed expressions naturally, and one creative twist on a well-known expression in the title.

**Model:**

> Home Sweet Office  
>   
> When my company announced that we would be working from home "for the foreseeable future", I was, first and foremost, delighted. No more traffic, no more crowded lifts, no more colleagues reheating fish in the office microwave. Peace and quiet at last.  
>   
> That was three years ago. By and large, I don't regret it. But I have learned that working from home has its ups and downs, and that some of them are not entirely obvious at first.  
>   
> Take the kitchen table. In theory, it's a temporary workspace. In practice, it has become my office lock, stock and barrel: laptop, second screen, a mountain of papers and an assortment of odds and ends that I'm afraid to throw away. My family now eats dinner on the sofa.  
>   
> Then there are the interruptions. Unexpected deliveries, the neighbour's renovations and a cat who believes my keyboard is a heated bed are, it seems, part and parcel of the arrangement. Last month, during an important video call with a client, it was touch and go whether I would finish my presentation before the drilling next door began. (It began.)  
>   
> The biggest surprise, however, has been loneliness. Real conversations with colleagues are now few and far between. I never imagined I would miss Paulo from accounts and his endless stories about his weekend fishing trips. But I do.  
>   
> So, after weighing up the pros and cons, I've reached a compromise. Twice a week, I go to a co-working space. It's rough and ready — the coffee is terrible — but there are people, and conversation, and nobody is drilling.  
>   
> Is working from home the be-all and end-all? Not quite. But sooner or later, I suspect, most of us will find our own balance. Meanwhile, the cat has the keyboard.
