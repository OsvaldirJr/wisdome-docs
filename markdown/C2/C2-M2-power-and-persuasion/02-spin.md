## 🧩 Grammar: The language of spin

**1. Eufemismos** — palavras suaves para realidades duras:
- **Trabalho:** downsizing, rightsizing, restructuring, letting people go, workforce adjustment, "exploring other opportunities" (= demitido)
- **Economia:** negative growth (= recessão), revenue enhancement (= aumento de impostos), price adjustment (= aumento de preço), correction (= queda)
- **Guerra:** collateral damage, neutralize, friendly fire, enhanced interrogation
- **Vida pessoal:** passed away, between jobs, economically disadvantaged
⚠️ Nem todo eufemismo é manipulação: "passed away" é **delicadeza**; "collateral damage" pode **desumanizar**.

**2. Weasel words** — criam a **impressão** de uma afirmação sem afirmar nada verificável:
- **Fontes vagas:** "Some experts say...", "Studies show...", "It is widely believed...", "Many people are saying..."
- **Qualificadores:** "**up to** 70% off" (pode ser 5%), "**helps** reduce" (não diz quanto), "**virtually** invisible", "**can** improve", "**as much as**"
- **Comparações incompletas:** "**40% more** effective" (do que o quê?)

**3. Gramática para ocultar o agente:**
- **Passiva sem agente:** "Mistakes **were made**." / "The data **was lost**." / "Shots **were fired**."
- **Nominalização:** "There has been **a reduction in staff**" (em vez de "We fired 300 people")
- **Verbos ergativos** (sem agente): "The project **failed**." / "Prices **rose**." / "The ship **sank**."
- **Sujeitos abstratos:** "**The situation** has led to job losses."

**4. O falso pedido de desculpas (non-apology):**
- "I'm sorry **if** anyone was offended." (coloca a culpa em quem se ofendeu)
- "I regret that **you feel** that way."
- "Mistakes were made, **but** ..."
- Um pedido real: "**I** was wrong. **I** apologize for **what I said**. **It** won't happen again."

**Antídoto C2:** pergunte sempre **quem fez o quê, a quem, quanto e segundo quem?**

| Spin | Linguagem clara |
|---|---|
| We are rightsizing our workforce. | We are firing employees. |
| Mistakes were made. | I/We made mistakes. |
| negative growth | recession / the economy shrank |
| up to 70% off | some items are discounted |
| Studies show... | Which studies? Who did them? |
| I'm sorry if you were offended. | I'm sorry for what I said. |
| price adjustment | price increase |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M2-L2/example-01.m4a) The company announced a "strategic workforce realignment", which, stripped of the jargon, meant that 400 people would lose their jobs. — _A empresa anunciou um "realinhamento estratégico da força de trabalho", o que, sem o jargão, significava que 400 pessoas perderiam o emprego._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M2-L2/example-02.m4a) "Mistakes were made" is a classic example of the passive voice being used to avoid responsibility. — _"Erros foram cometidos" é um exemplo clássico da voz passiva sendo usada para evitar responsabilidade._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M2-L2/example-03.m4a) The advert claims the cream "helps reduce the appearance of wrinkles" — a sentence carefully designed to promise almost nothing. — _O anúncio afirma que o creme "ajuda a reduzir a aparência de rugas" — uma frase cuidadosamente construída para prometer quase nada._

> 💡 **Dica:** Em português também temos: "**colaboradores desligados**" (demitidos), "**ajuste tarifário**" (aumento), "**comunidade**" (às vezes para evitar "favela"). Perceber o eufemismo nas duas línguas é sinal de leitura crítica madura.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ Treating "Studies show..." as a reliable source.  
  ✅ **Ask: which studies, by whom, published where?**  
  Weasel words imitam autoridade sem fornecer evidência.
- ❌ I'm sorry if you were upset by my comment. (as a real apology)  
  ✅ **I'm sorry for my comment. It was wrong.**  
  Um pedido real assume responsabilidade, sem "if".
- ❌ Using "collateral damage" in a neutral news report about civilian deaths.  
  ✅ **Use clear language: "civilians were killed".**  
  Eufemismos de guerra podem desumanizar vítimas.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Ironia ao citar spin

Ao citar um eufemismo de forma crítica, falantes nativos usam um tom **ligeiramente irônico**, muitas vezes com **air quotes** (aspas no ar) ou expressões como "so-called": "They're not firing people, they're... *rightsizing*." / "the **so-called** 'price adjustment'".

<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Professor:** In 1946, George Orwell published an essay called "Politics and the English Language". His argument was that political language is often designed to make the indefensible sound reasonable. He gave examples from his own time: villages bombed and inhabitants driven out were described as "pacification". Rather than describe brutal events directly, officials reached for abstract, Latinate words that created a kind of fog. Orwell also attacked lazy writing more generally — dying metaphors, pretentious vocabulary, unnecessary passives — because he believed that sloppy language makes sloppy thinking easier. He ended with six rules, the most famous of which is probably: "Never use a long word where a short one will do." He was also honest enough to add a final rule allowing writers to break any of the others rather than say anything outright barbarous. Almost eighty years later, the essay is still widely read, and its central warning still applies. When language stops describing reality and starts concealing it, democracy has a problem.

## 📖 Reading — The anatomy of a non-apology [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M2-L2/reading.m4a)

Few genres of public communication have been studied as closely, or mocked as mercilessly, as the corporate apology. When a company is caught polluting a river, mistreating customers or leaking personal data, its statement tends to follow a predictable pattern — one that linguists and public relations experts have learned to dissect. It typically begins with an expression of regret, rather than an apology: "We regret any inconvenience this may have caused." The verb "regret" is significant. One can regret a storm; regret does not imply responsibility. The phrase "any inconvenience" minimizes the harm, while "may have caused" leaves open the possibility that nothing was caused at all. Next comes the passive voice. "Mistakes were made" and "data was accessed" describe events without naming anyone who performed them. The agent — the company, its executives, a specific decision — quietly vanishes from the sentence. Then there is the conditional apology: "We apologize to anyone who was affected" or "We're sorry if customers felt let down." By placing the problem in the reactions of others, the statement implies that the fault lies, at least partly, in their sensitivity. Finally, the statement moves swiftly to the future: "We are committed to the highest standards and are reviewing our processes." The past, with all its awkward details, is left behind. Research on apologies suggests that the most effective ones do almost exactly the opposite. They acknowledge responsibility clearly, name the specific harm, express genuine remorse, explain what went wrong and offer concrete repair. They are short, specific and active. "We broke your trust. Here is what we did, here is why it happened, and here is what we will do to put it right." Such apologies are rare, not because they are hard to write, but because they are hard to say. They require something the language of spin is designed to avoid: accepting the blame.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — "Economical with the truth"

A expressão **"economical with the truth"** ficou famosa em 1986, quando o alto funcionário britânico **Sir Robert Armstrong** a usou num tribunal australiano para descrever uma carta enganosa. Hoje é um eufemismo irônico para "mentir por omissão". Outras expressões semelhantes: **"terminological inexactitude"** (Churchill, 1906) e **"alternative facts"** (2017).

## ✅ Resumo
- Eufemismos: rightsizing, letting go, negative growth, collateral damage.
- Weasel words: some experts say, up to, helps, virtually, X% more (than what?).
- Ocultar o agente: passiva sem agente, nominalização, verbos ergativos.
- Pedido real: I + responsabilidade + dano específico + reparação; sem "if".
