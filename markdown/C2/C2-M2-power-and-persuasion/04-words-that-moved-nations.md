## 🧩 Grammar: Rhetorical figures

**1. Paralelismo** — estruturas gramaticais **iguais** em sequência (a base de quase todas as figuras):
- ❌ "She likes swimming, to run and cycles." ✅ "She likes **swimming, running and cycling**."
- "We will **build** schools, **train** teachers and **support** families."

**2. Tricolon** (C1, revisão): três elementos paralelos, geralmente com o último mais longo ou forte.
- "government **of** the people, **by** the people, **for** the people" (Lincoln, 1863)

**3. Anáfora** — repetição no **início** de frases/orações:
- "**We shall fight** on the beaches, **we shall fight** on the landing grounds, **we shall fight** in the fields..." (Churchill, 1940)

**4. Epístrofe** — repetição no **final**:
- "...government of the people, by the **people**, for the **people**."
- "When I was a child, I spoke as a **child**, I understood as a **child**, I thought as a **child**." (Bíblia, King James)

**5. Antítese** — ideias opostas em estrutura paralela:
- "...**not because** they are **easy**, **but because** they are **hard**." (Kennedy, 1962)
- "That's one small step for a man, one giant leap for mankind." (Armstrong, 1969)

**6. Quiasmo** — repetição **invertida** (A-B / B-A):
- "Ask not what **your country** can do for **you** — ask what **you** can do for **your country**." (Kennedy, 1961)
- "**Eat** to **live**, not **live** to **eat**."
- "It's not the **years** in your **life**, it's the **life** in your **years**."

**7. Aliteração e assonância** — repetição de sons: "**b**igger, **b**etter, **b**older"; "**p**eace, **p**rosperity and **p**rogress"

**8. Perguntas retóricas e pausas** (C1, revisão).

**Gramática do paralelismo:** ao listar ações, mantenha a **mesma forma** (todos verbos base, todos -ing, todos substantivos); ao contrastar, use **estruturas idênticas**: "not X but Y", "less X, more Y", "the more X, the more Y".

| Figura | Padrão | Exemplo |
|---|---|---|
| Anáfora | A... / A... / A... | We shall fight... we shall fight... |
| Epístrofe | ...B / ...B / ...B | ...by the people, for the people |
| Antítese | not X but Y | Not because it is easy, but because it is hard. |
| Quiasmo | A-B / B-A | Ask not what your country can do for you... |
| Tricolon | X, Y and Z | Veni, vidi, vici. |
| Aliteração | mesmo som inicial | safer, stronger, smarter |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M2-L4/example-01.m4a) We do not need more promises; we need more action. We do not need more speeches; we need more schools. — _Não precisamos de mais promessas; precisamos de mais ação. Não precisamos de mais discursos; precisamos de mais escolas._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M2-L4/example-02.m4a) Education is not the filling of a pail, but the lighting of a fire — a line often attributed to W. B. Yeats, though its origin is uncertain. — _Educação não é encher um balde, mas acender um fogo — frase muitas vezes atribuída a W. B. Yeats, embora sua origem seja incerta._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M2-L4/example-03.m4a) We can choose to build bridges together, or we can choose to build walls alone. — _Podemos escolher construir pontes juntos, ou podemos escolher construir muros sozinhos._

> 💡 **Dica:** Discursos brasileiros também usam essas figuras — de Rui Barbosa aos sambas-enredo. Ao escrever em inglês, comece pelo **paralelismo**: ele torna frases mais claras **e** mais memoráveis. As figuras mais sofisticadas (quiasmo, epístrofe) funcionam melhor **uma vez**, no momento de maior impacto.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ We want to build schools, training teachers and support for families.  
  ✅ **We want to build schools, train teachers and support families.**  
  Paralelismo: mesma forma gramatical em todos os itens.
- ❌ Not because it is easy, but it is hard.  
  ✅ **Not because it is easy, but because it is hard.**  
  Antítese exige estruturas idênticas nos dois lados.
- ❌ Using chiasmus in every sentence of a speech.  
  ✅ **Use it once, at the climax.**  
  Figuras fortes perdem impacto quando repetidas.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Cadência oratória

Em discursos, as figuras retóricas dependem de **ritmo**: pausas iguais entre elementos paralelos, ênfase nas palavras repetidas ou contrastadas, e **desaceleração** na frase final. "Not because it is EASY | but because it is HARD." / "of the PEOple | by the PEOple | for the PEOple."

<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Historian:** On 19 November 1863, at the dedication of a military cemetery in Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, Abraham Lincoln spoke for around two minutes. The main speaker that day, the celebrated orator Edward Everett, had spoken for about two hours. It is Lincoln's speech that is remembered. Why? Partly because of its brevity — fewer than three hundred words — and partly because of its extraordinary craftsmanship. Lincoln uses antithesis repeatedly: the world "will little note, nor long remember what we say here, but it can never forget what they did here." He uses a tricolon of verbs: "we can not dedicate — we can not consecrate — we can not hallow — this ground." And he ends with perhaps the most famous example of epistrophe in American history, describing a government "of the people, by the people, for the people". Notice, too, the irony of that first antithesis: Lincoln claimed the world would not remember his words. Rarely has a prediction been so completely wrong.

## 📖 Reading — Why rhetoric still matters [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M2-L4/reading.m4a)

For much of Western history, rhetoric — the art of persuasive speech — stood at the centre of education. Roman schoolboys practised declamation; medieval universities taught it as one of the three foundational arts, alongside grammar and logic; and well into the nineteenth century, educated people were expected to recognize, and to use, figures such as anaphora and chiasmus. In the twentieth century, however, rhetoric acquired a bad name. After decades of wartime propaganda and, later, the polished manipulations of advertising, the word came to suggest empty or deceptive speech: "mere rhetoric". Many schools quietly dropped it from the curriculum. This is unfortunate, for two reasons. First, the techniques of rhetoric have not disappeared; they have simply become less visible. Every political speech, advertising campaign and viral post draws on the same toolkit that Cicero and Quintilian described two thousand years ago. Citizens who cannot recognize these techniques are, in a sense, defenceless against them. To identify a false antithesis — "either you are with us or you are against us" — is already to begin to resist it. Second, rhetoric is not only a weapon of the powerful. It has also been the instrument of those seeking to challenge power. Abolitionists, suffragists and civil rights leaders were, almost without exception, masters of persuasive language; their speeches combined moral clarity with carefully crafted rhythm, repetition and contrast. To dismiss rhetoric as manipulation is, in effect, to give up one of the most effective tools of democratic change. Perhaps the solution is to return rhetoric to its original place: not as a set of tricks but as a discipline of both speaking and listening. A society that teaches its citizens to recognize persuasion, to analyse it and to use it responsibly is far better equipped to govern itself than one that merely hopes its leaders will speak honestly.

## 📝 Writing

Write a speech (300–350 words) for an occasion of your choice (a graduation, an inauguration, a protest, an award ceremony). Use at least six rhetorical figures, including chiasmus, anaphora and antithesis, and write a short note (2–3 lines) identifying them.

**Model:**

> Opening of the Vila Esperança Community Library  
>   
> Friends, neighbours, children of Vila Esperança,  
>   
> Three years ago, on this very spot, there was nothing but a broken wall and a pile of rubbish. Today, there are four thousand books.  
>   
> They told us it was impossible. They told us there was no money. They told us that nobody here would read. Look around you. Every shelf in this room is an answer to them.  
>   
> This library was not built by the government, nor by a company, nor by a single generous donor. It was built by you: by the bricklayer who gave his weekends, by the grandmother who organized the book drive, by the teenagers who painted these walls. It was built not with money, but with time; not with promises, but with hands.  
>   
> Some people say that books belong to the past — that our children need screens, not stories. I say they need both. But a screen can show you the world; a book can show you yourself.  
>   
> Here, a child who has never left this neighbourhood can travel to the Amazon, to Lisbon, to the moon. Here, an adult who never finished school can finally finish a book. Here, every one of us can find a quiet place in a noisy world.  
>   
> We did not build this library because it was easy. We built it because it was necessary.  
>   
> So, to the children of Vila Esperança, I have just one request. Don't just read the books in this room — one day, write the books that will fill it. Because a community that reads can change its story; a community that writes its story can change the world.  
>   
> Welcome to your library.  
>   
> — Figures used: anaphora ("They told us...", "Here..."), tricolon (bricklayer, grandmother, teenagers), antithesis (not with money, but with time), chiasmus ("a community that reads can change its story; a community that writes its story..."), parallel antithesis ("not because it was easy... because it was necessary").
