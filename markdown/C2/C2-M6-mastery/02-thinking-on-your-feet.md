## 🔥 Warm-up
Falantes nativos também travam, esquecem palavras e erram — a diferença é que **se recuperam sem que ninguém perceba**. No C2, fluência não é ausência de hesitação: é **gerenciar** a conversa — entrar, manter a palavra, reformular, desviar de uma pergunta difícil, corrigir-se com elegância. Tema: **debate e improviso**.

- What do you do when you forget a word in English?
- How do you feel when someone asks you a difficult question in public?

## 🧩 Grammar: Managing spoken interaction

**1. Interromper com elegância:**
- "**Sorry to cut in, but**..." / "**Can I just come in here?**" / "**If I could just add something**..."
- "**Just to pick up on what you said about**..."

**2. Manter a palavra (holding the floor):**
- "**If I could just finish (my point)**..." / "**Let me just finish this thought**..." / "**Bear with me** a second..."
- "**I'll come to that in a moment.**"

**3. Ganhar tempo naturalmente:**
- "**That's a really interesting question.**" / "**Let me think about that for a second.**" / "**How can I put this?**" / "**Well, it depends what you mean by**..."
- Fillers naturais (com moderação): well, I mean, you know, sort of, the thing is

**4. Reformular e corrigir-se:**
- "**What I meant to say was**..." / "**Or rather**..." / "**Let me rephrase that.**" / "**Sorry, I mean**..."
- "He was born in 1985 — **sorry, 1958**."
- Autocorreção rápida mostra **controle**, não fraqueza.

**5. Recuperar o fio:**
- "**Where was I?**" / "**I've lost my train of thought.**" / "**Anyway, to get back to the main point**..." / "**As I was saying**..."

**6. Lidar com perguntas difíceis:**
- **Reconhecer:** "**That's a fair question.**"
- **Reformular a pergunta:** "**If I understand the question correctly, you're asking whether**..."
- **Admitir limites:** "**I don't know the exact figure, but**..." / "**I'd need to check that.**"
- **Redirecionar (com honestidade):** "**I think the more important question is**..."
- **Discordar da premissa:** "**I'm not sure I'd accept the premise of that question.**"

**7. Passar a palavra:**
- "**What's your take on this?**" / "**I'd be interested to hear what you think.**" / "**Over to you.**"

- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M6-L2/example-01.m4a) Sorry to cut in, but I think we're confusing two different issues here. — _Desculpe interromper, mas acho que estamos confundindo duas questões diferentes aqui._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M6-L2/example-02.m4a) The project will cost around two million — or rather, two million for the first phase alone. — _O projeto vai custar cerca de dois milhões — ou melhor, dois milhões só na primeira fase._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M6-L2/example-03.m4a) That's a fair question, and I don't have the exact figure, but I'll find out and get back to you. — _É uma pergunta justa, e não tenho o número exato, mas vou verificar e te dou um retorno._

> 💡 **Dica:** Brasileiros tendem a interromper mais (sobreposição de falas é comum e até sinal de engajamento). Em reuniões em inglês, especialmente com britânicos e nórdicos, use **frases de transição** antes de interromper — e espere uma **micropausa**. E nunca tenha medo de dizer "**I don't know**": no C2, isso soa confiante.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ Silence when you forget a word.  
  ✅ **"How can I put this... it's the thing you use to..."**  
  Parafrasear mantém a fluência.
- ❌ Pretending to know an answer you don't know.  
  ✅ **"That's a good question. I don't know offhand, but I'll check."**  
  Admitir limites com segurança gera mais credibilidade.
- ❌ Interrupting directly: "No, no, no, you're wrong."  
  ✅ **"Sorry to cut in, but I see it a bit differently."**  
  Interrupções diretas soam agressivas em inglês.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Fillers e autocorreção

Fillers em inglês ("well", "I mean", "um") são diferentes dos brasileiros ("é...", "tipo...", "né"). Substitua o "éééé" por **"um..." / "er..."** e o "né?" por **"right?" / "you know?"**. Na autocorreção, use um tom rápido e leve: "1985 — sorry, 1958".

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Coach:** Most people fear impromptu questions more than prepared speeches. The good news is that answering well off the cuff is a skill you can practise. Here's a simple structure I teach. First, pause. A two-second silence feels like an eternity to you, but to the audience it signals that you're taking the question seriously. Second, acknowledge the question: "That's an important point." Third, give your answer in one clear sentence — the headline. Fourth, support it with a reason or an example. And fifth, stop. The biggest mistake people make isn't saying too little; it's saying too much, circling around until they've lost their train of thought. If you genuinely don't know, say so: "I don't know, but here's how I'd find out." Paradoxically, admitting uncertainty with composure often makes you seem more confident, not less. Remember, the audience isn't hoping you'll fail. They want you to rise to the occasion.

## 📖 Reading — Fluency is not perfection [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M6-L2/reading.m4a)

Ask a group of advanced learners what fluency means and many will describe something close to perfection: speaking without hesitation, without errors, without ever searching for a word. By that standard, very few native speakers would qualify. Recordings of spontaneous conversation reveal that native speakers hesitate constantly. They start sentences and abandon them, repeat words, correct themselves mid-phrase and fill pauses with "um", "you know" and "I mean". Research on spoken grammar, drawing on large collections of real conversation, has shown that much of everyday speech consists of fragments and false starts that would be marked as errors in a written exam. Why, then, do native speakers sound fluent? Part of the answer is that they manage these disruptions smoothly. Their hesitations fall in natural places — before an important word, rather than in the middle of a fixed phrase. Their self-corrections are quick and unselfconscious. They have a large repertoire of chunks and strategies that keep the conversation moving while they think: "the thing is", "how can I put it", "what I'm trying to say is". In other words, fluency is not the absence of difficulty but the ability to handle it. This has important implications for learners. Many advanced students, anxious to avoid mistakes, adopt strategies that actually make them sound less fluent: they speak slowly and carefully, avoid complex structures and fall silent when they cannot find the perfect word. Paradoxically, they would sound more natural if they allowed themselves to hesitate, rephrase and correct themselves as native speakers do. The goal at the highest level, then, is not to eliminate imperfection but to make it invisible — or, better still, irrelevant. A speaker who loses a word and says, without embarrassment, "What's the word I'm looking for... the thing you use to open a bottle?" has not failed. She has demonstrated exactly the kind of flexibility that defines mastery.

**Questions:**
1. What do many advanced learners think fluency means?
2. What do recordings of native speakers reveal?
3. Why do native speakers still sound fluent?
4. What paradox does the text describe about anxious learners?

>

>

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — Debate e improviso

No mundo anglófono, **debate clubs** e **improv** (teatro de improviso) são populares em escolas e universidades como forma de desenvolver a capacidade de **pensar rápido**. Competições como o **World Universities Debating Championship** reúnem estudantes do mundo todo — inclusive brasileiros. Fazer aulas de improv em inglês é uma ótima (e divertida) forma de ganhar fluência C2.
