## 🧩 Grammar: The language of mediation

**1. Reformular e simplificar:**
- "**In other words**, ..." / "**To put it simply / To put it another way**, ..."
- "**What this means (in practice) is that**..." (cleft — útil para destacar a consequência)
- "**Basically / Essentially**, ..." (na fala)
- "**The key point / The bottom line is**..."

**2. Analogias e comparações:**
- "**Think of it as / It's a bit like**..." / "**Imagine**..."
- "**It works in much the same way as**..."
- "**If X is a..., then Y is a...**" (analogia estruturada)

**3. Decompor (signposting):**
- "**Let me break that down.** **First**, ... **Then**, ... **Finally**, ..."
- "**There are three things you need to know.**"

**4. Verificar compreensão e convidar perguntas:**
- "**Does that make sense (so far)?**" / "**Is that clear?**" / "**Shall I go over that again?**"
- Evite "Do you understand?" (pode soar condescendente) — prefira "**Have I explained that clearly?**" (assume a responsabilidade)

**5. Mediar entre pessoas (relaying and clarifying):**
- "**What I think she's saying is**..." / "**If I understand you correctly, you mean**..."
- "**Both of you seem to agree that**...; **the difference is**..."
- "**Could you say a bit more about**...?"

**6. Princípios:**
- **Começar pelo que o público já sabe.**
- **Um conceito de cada vez.**
- **Exemplos concretos** antes de definições abstratas.
- Evitar a **"curse of knowledge"** (esquecer como é não saber).
- **Simplificar sem distorcer** (não "dumb down").

| Função | Expressões |
|---|---|
| Reformular | In other words / To put it simply / What this means is... |
| Analogia | Think of it as... / It's a bit like... / Imagine... |
| Decompor | Let me break that down. First... Then... |
| Verificar | Does that make sense? / Have I explained that clearly? |
| Mediar pessoas | What I think she means is... / If I understand you correctly... |
| Resumir | The bottom line is... / So, in short... |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M6-L1/example-01.m4a) Think of the immune system as a security team: vaccines are like showing the guards a photograph of the intruder before he arrives. — _Pense no sistema imunológico como uma equipe de segurança: vacinas são como mostrar aos guardas uma foto do invasor antes de ele chegar._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M6-L1/example-02.m4a) What this clause means in practice is that you can't move out before the end of the year without paying a penalty. — _O que esta cláusula significa na prática é que você não pode sair antes do fim do ano sem pagar multa._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M6-L1/example-03.m4a) If I understand you correctly, you're not against the project — you're worried about the timing. — _Se entendi bem, você não é contra o projeto — está preocupado com o cronograma._

> 💡 **Dica:** Um bom teste de mediação: **explique a ideia como se fosse para uma criança de 12 anos** — sem jargão, com um exemplo e uma analogia. Se conseguir, você realmente entendeu. (Essa técnica é frequentemente associada ao físico **Richard Feynman**.)

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ Do you understand? Do you understand? (repeatedly)  
  ✅ **Does that make sense so far? / Have I explained that clearly?**  
  Perguntar repetidamente "Do you understand?" pode soar condescendente.
- ❌ Explaining with even more jargon: "It's a stochastic, non-linear process."  
  ✅ **Use an analogy and a concrete example.**  
  A curse of knowledge faz especialistas esquecerem o que o público não sabe.
- ❌ Oversimplifying until it's wrong: "Vaccines make you immune to everything."  
  ✅ **Simplify without distorting: "Vaccines train your body to recognize a specific virus."**  
  Simplificar não pode significar falsear.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Ritmo explicativo

Ao explicar algo complexo, fale um pouco **mais devagar**, faça **pausas** depois de cada ideia nova e **acentue** as palavras-chave. Antes de uma analogia, uma pequena pausa ajuda: "Think of it as... | a security team."

## 🎧 Listening — The curse of knowledge

_Uma psicóloga explica por que especialistas têm dificuldade em explicar o que sabem._

**Listen first, without reading.** [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M6-L1/listening-full.m4a)

**Questions:**
1. What did the tappers have to do?
2. How often did tappers predict listeners would guess correctly, and how often did they actually?
3. What is the curse of knowledge?
4. What cure does the speaker suggest?

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Psychologist:** In 1990, a Stanford graduate student named Elizabeth Newton ran a simple experiment. She divided participants into "tappers" and "listeners". The tappers were asked to tap out the rhythm of a well-known song, like "Happy Birthday", on a table, and the listeners had to guess the song. Before starting, the tappers predicted that listeners would guess correctly about half the time. In reality, listeners identified only around 1 in 40 songs. Why the huge gap? Because when the tappers tapped, they heard the melody in their heads. They simply couldn't imagine what it was like to hear only the knocking. This is what's often called the curse of knowledge: once we know something, it's extremely difficult to remember what it's like not to know it. Experts fall into this trap constantly. They skip steps, use jargon and assume background knowledge that their audience doesn't have. The cure isn't to know less. It's to test your explanations on real people — and to watch their faces, not your slides.

</details>

## 📖 Reading — The rise of mediation [🔊](../../../audio/C2/C2-M6-L1/reading.m4a)

For decades, language teaching tended to focus on four skills: reading, writing, listening and speaking. When the Council of Europe published an expanded version of the Common European Framework of Reference in 2020 — the so-called Companion Volume — it gave unprecedented prominence to a further area: mediation. In the framework's terms, mediation involves acting as an intermediary, making communication possible between people who, for linguistic, cultural or other reasons, would otherwise struggle to understand one another. This might mean relaying information from a text in one language to someone who speaks another, explaining complex data to a non-specialist, or facilitating a discussion between colleagues with conflicting views. The shift reflects a broader recognition of how language is actually used in the modern world. Few professionals simply receive and produce messages; most spend a considerable part of their day processing information from one source and adapting it for another audience. A lawyer explains a contract to a client; a nurse translates medical instructions into everyday language; a project manager summarizes a technical report for the board; a bilingual employee bridges the gap between a foreign head office and a local team. In each case, success depends not on knowing the most sophisticated words but on choosing the right words for the right listener. At the highest levels of proficiency, the CEFR descriptors for mediation are notably demanding. A C2 user is expected to be able to explain, in clear and fluent language, the way facts and arguments are presented in a text, to convey subtle nuances of attitude and opinion, and to adapt their language flexibly to different audiences. In other words, mastery of a language is no longer defined only by what one can understand and say, but by what one can help others to understand. It is, perhaps, the most generous definition of fluency yet devised.

**Questions:**
1. What did the 2020 CEFR Companion Volume emphasize?
2. What does mediation involve, according to the framework?
3. Give two professional examples of mediation from the text.
4. How does the text redefine mastery of a language?

## 📝 Writing

Mediation task (280–320 words): Choose a complex document from your field (a policy, a scientific finding, a contract, a technical process) and write an explanatory article for a general audience, using analogies, examples and reformulation. Assume readers have no background in the subject.

**Model:**

> What is a credit score — and why should you care?  
>   
> If you've ever applied for a loan, a credit card or even a mobile phone contract, a number you've probably never seen may have decided the outcome: your credit score.  
>   
> To put it simply, a credit score is a rating that estimates how likely you are to repay money you borrow. Think of it as a kind of reputation, expressed as a number. Just as a restaurant with many good reviews attracts more customers, a person with a high score is seen by banks as a safer bet.  
>   
> How is it calculated? Let me break that down. Credit bureaus — companies that collect financial information — look at several factors. The most important is your payment history: do you pay your bills on time? Next comes how much you owe compared to your available credit. If you regularly use your entire credit card limit, that may suggest you're under financial pressure. Other factors include how long you've had credit and how often you apply for new loans.  
>   
> What this means in practice is that your everyday habits matter. Paying an electricity bill late may seem trivial, but it can lower your score — and that, in turn, may mean higher interest rates when you need a loan for a car or a house.  
>   
> The good news is that a score is not fixed. Like physical fitness, it improves with consistent, sensible habits: paying on time, avoiding using all your credit, and not applying for many loans at once. In Brazil, consumers are entitled to check their score and the information used to calculate it, usually free of charge.  
>   
> In short, your credit score is a quiet but powerful number. Understanding it won't make you rich — but it may save you a great deal of money, and a few unpleasant surprises.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — Intérpretes e mediadores culturais

Em hospitais, tribunais e serviços públicos de países anglófonos, **community interpreters** ajudam imigrantes a se comunicar — e muitos trabalham também como **mediadores culturais**, explicando diferenças de costumes. No Brasil, com a chegada de migrantes e refugiados, cresce a demanda por mediadores em português, espanhol, crioulo haitiano e árabe. A mediação, como mostra o CEFR, é uma habilidade de **cidadania**.
