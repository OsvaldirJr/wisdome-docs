## 🧩 Grammar: C1 review and exam strategies

**Revisão das estruturas do C1:**
- **Nuance verbal:** stative no continuous, I was wondering, always + continuous
- **Coesão:** elipse e substituição (so, not, do so, one), referência (this issue, such, the former/latter)
- **Ênfase:** do/did, fronting (Strange as it may seem), so/such + inversão, cleft sentences (What..., All..., It's not that...)
- **Inversão:** Not only..., Little did..., No sooner..., Only when..., Should/Were/Had
- **Registro formal:** nominalização, subjuntivo, passivas avançadas, distanciamento (is alleged to have)
- **Modais e futuro:** might well, be bound to, may as well, be set to, be on the verge of, be to
- **Léxico:** formação de palavras, multi-word verbs abstratos, collocations, comment adverbs, vague language, discourse markers
- **Pragmática:** linguagem diplomática, hedging, understatement, troca de registro

**Cambridge C1 Advanced** (4 partes):
- **Reading and Use of English** (1h30, 8 partes): múltipla escolha de vocabulário, lacunas, **formação de palavras**, **key word transformations**, textos longos
- **Writing** (1h30): **essay** obrigatório (220–260 palavras) + uma tarefa à escolha (carta/e-mail, proposta, relatório ou resenha)
- **Listening** (cerca de 40 min, 4 partes)
- **Speaking** (cerca de 15 min, **em dupla**): entrevista, comparação de fotos, tarefa colaborativa, discussão

**IELTS:** C1 corresponde aproximadamente às bandas **7.0–8.0**. Speaking com 3 partes (11–14 min), Writing com 2 tarefas (a Task 2 com no mínimo 250 palavras).

**Estratégias C1:**
1. **Reading:** leia **perguntas antes do texto**; cuidado com **distractors** (palavras do texto usadas com outro sentido); procure **paráfrases**, não palavras idênticas.
2. **Use of English:** em key word transformations, procure a **estrutura testada** (inversão, passiva, phrasal verb, expressão fixa); a palavra dada **não pode mudar**; conte as palavras (3–6, contrações contam como duas).
3. **Writing:** **planeje 5–10 minutos**; responda **todos os pontos**; ajuste o **registro** ao leitor; mostre **variedade** sem forçar; revise erros "bobos" (artigos, preposições, -s).
4. **Listening:** antecipe o conteúdo pelas perguntas; atenção a **atitudes e opiniões** (Part 3/4), não só fatos.
5. **Speaking:** **desenvolva** (resposta + razão + exemplo + contraste); **interaja** com o parceiro ("I'd like to pick up on what you said..."); **parafraseie** em vez de parar; mostre **variedade** naturalmente.

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M6-L4/example-01.m4a) Not only has my English improved, but I've also become far more confident speaking in public. — _Não só meu inglês melhorou, como também fiquei muito mais confiante para falar em público._

> 💡 **Dica:** No C1, **pequenos erros são aceitáveis** — o que conta é a **flexibilidade, a precisão e a variedade**. Um examinador prefere ver você **arriscar** uma inversão com um pequeno deslize do que repetir estruturas B1 perfeitas.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ Key word transformation: changing the given word (ALTHOUGH → although's).  
  ✅ **Use the given word exactly as it is.**  
  A palavra dada não pode ser alterada.
- ❌ Essay: discussing all three options and not choosing one.  
  ✅ **Discuss two options and clearly state which is more important.**  
  Não responder parte da tarefa reduz a nota de conteúdo.
- ❌ Speaking: giving one-sentence answers to avoid mistakes.  
  ✅ **Develop: answer + reason + example + contrast.**  
  No C1, a extensão e variedade das respostas são avaliadas.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Fluência C1: prosódia

No C1, o objetivo não é perder o sotaque, mas ter **prosódia clara**: acento correto nas palavras, ênfase nas informações importantes, pausas nos lugares certos e entonação que mostra atitude. Grave-se e compare com falantes nativos — foque em **ritmo** mais do que em sons isolados.

## 📖 Reading — The plateau and how to break it [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M6-L4/reading.m4a)

Almost every advanced learner eventually encounters what teachers call the "intermediate plateau" — and its more subtle cousin, the advanced plateau. In the early stages of learning a language, progress is fast and obvious: every week brings new words, new structures and new abilities. At higher levels, however, the gains become smaller and harder to notice. Learners who could once measure their improvement in leaps now find it difficult to see any improvement at all. Part of the explanation is that the remaining gaps are less visible. A B1 learner knows when she does not understand a sentence. A C1 learner usually understands, but may not notice that a native speaker would have chosen a different collocation, a more precise verb or a subtler level of politeness. Such gaps rarely cause communication to break down, which means there is little pressure to fix them. Research on expertise offers some useful insights. The psychologist Anders Ericsson, whose work on expert performance became widely known, argued that improvement at advanced levels depends less on the amount of practice than on its quality. What he called "deliberate practice" involves working on specific weaknesses, at the edge of one's ability, with immediate feedback. For language learners, this might mean recording yourself and comparing your speech with a model, rewriting a paragraph three times in different registers, or noting down every unfamiliar collocation you meet in a week. Equally important is motivation. At C1, the external rewards — passing an exam, getting a job — have often already been achieved. Learners who continue to improve tend to be those who have found intrinsic reasons: a love of literature, an international friendship, a professional passion. In the end, the plateau is not a wall but a change of terrain. The climb continues; it simply requires a different kind of walking.

**Questions:**
1. Why does progress seem slower at advanced levels?
2. What is "deliberate practice"?
3. Give two examples of deliberate practice for language learners.

## 📝 Writing

**Model:**

> What really makes a successful language learner?  
>   
> Why do some people master a foreign language while others, despite years of study, never progress beyond the basics? Of the many factors involved, formal instruction and personal motivation are arguably two of the most influential.  
>   
> Formal instruction provides structure. A good teacher can explain grammar systematically, correct persistent errors and expose learners to language they might never encounter on their own. Without such guidance, many learners develop a fluent but inaccurate style which becomes increasingly difficult to correct. That said, instruction alone rarely produces proficiency. Countless students have spent years in classrooms and yet remain unable to hold a conversation.  
>   
> Personal motivation, by contrast, determines what happens outside the classroom. Learners who genuinely want to read a novel, make a friend or build a career in another language tend to seek out opportunities to practise, and to persist when progress slows. Admittedly, motivation can fade, and enthusiasm is no substitute for knowledge. Nevertheless, it is motivation that turns a few hours of lessons a week into a daily habit of listening, reading and speaking.  
>   
> On balance, although both factors are valuable, I believe the latter is ultimately more important. Not only does motivation lead learners to find their own resources, but it also sustains them through the inevitable plateaus. Instruction can show the way; only motivation ensures that the learner keeps walking.
