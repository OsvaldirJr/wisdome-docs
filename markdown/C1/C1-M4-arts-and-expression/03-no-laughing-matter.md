## 🧩 Grammar: Comment adverbs and attitude

**Comment adverbs** (ou **sentence adverbs**) comentam a **frase inteira**, mostrando sua atitude. Geralmente vêm **no início**, seguidos de vírgula.

**1. Honestidade / franqueza:** frankly, honestly, to be honest, candidly, quite frankly
"**Frankly**, the show was a waste of money."

**2. Concessão:** admittedly, granted, of course, to be fair
"**Admittedly**, the jokes were a bit old. **To be fair**, the audience loved them."

**3. Opinião / grau de certeza:** arguably (possivelmente — defensável), apparently, presumably, supposedly (supostamente — com dúvida), undoubtedly, evidently, clearly
"It is **arguably** the funniest series of the decade."

**4. Reação emocional:** surprisingly, unsurprisingly, ironically, fortunately, sadly, luckily, oddly / strangely enough, predictably, understandably
"**Ironically**, the comedian who mocked technology became famous on TikTok."

**5. Óbvio:** needless to say, obviously, naturally, of course
"**Needless to say**, I never told that joke again."

**Understatement** (dizer menos do que se quer dizer) — típico do humor britânico:
- "It was **a bit** chilly." (= estava congelando)
- "He's **not the most** patient person." (= ele é muito impaciente)
- "That **could have gone better**." (= foi um desastre)
- "**Not bad**" pode significar "muito bom" — o tom decide.

**Ironia verbal** = dizer o contrário do que se pensa, geralmente com tom marcado:
- (Durante uma tempestade) "**Lovely** weather for a picnic!"
- **Sarcasmo** = ironia com intenção de criticar ou ferir: "Oh, **great** job, really."

⚠️ Ironia e sarcasmo **não funcionam bem por escrito** sem contexto — em e-mails profissionais, evite!

| Advérbio | Atitude | Exemplo |
|---|---|---|
| frankly / honestly | franqueza | Frankly, I didn't get the joke. |
| admittedly / to be fair | concessão | To be fair, it was his first show. |
| arguably | opinião defensável | Arguably her best work. |
| supposedly / apparently | dúvida / ouvi dizer | It's supposedly a comedy. |
| ironically | contradição | Ironically, the expert got it wrong. |
| needless to say | óbvio | Needless to say, I was late. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L3/example-01.m4a) Admittedly, some of the jokes haven't aged well. Even so, it's arguably one of the most influential sitcoms ever made. — _É verdade que algumas piadas envelheceram mal. Mesmo assim, é possivelmente uma das sitcoms mais influentes já feitas._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L3/example-02.m4a) The film is supposedly a comedy, but, quite frankly, I didn't laugh once. — _O filme supostamente é uma comédia, mas, francamente, eu não ri uma vez._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L3/example-03.m4a) Ironically, the meeting about improving communication was cancelled because nobody received the invitation. — _Ironicamente, a reunião sobre melhorar a comunicação foi cancelada porque ninguém recebeu o convite._

> 💡 **Dica:** **Arguably** NÃO é "discutivelmente" no sentido negativo! Significa "**pode-se argumentar que / possivelmente**": "It's **arguably** the best film" = muitos diriam que é o melhor. E **eventually** ≠ eventualmente (= finalmente).

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ Arguably, the film is terrible. (= é discutível)  
  ✅ **The film is arguably the best of the year. (= pode-se dizer que é)**  
  Arguably reforça uma opinião defensável, não a põe em dúvida.
- ❌ Needless to say, but I was late.  
  ✅ **Needless to say, I was late.**  
  Needless to say + frase direta, sem but.
- ❌ Ironically, it rained on my birthday. (simples azar)  
  ✅ **Ironically, the weather forecaster got caught in the storm.**  
  Ironically = uma contradição inesperada, não só falta de sorte.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Tom de ironia e understatement

A ironia em inglês costuma vir com entonação **exagerada** ou **completamente plana** (deadpan): "Oh, **lovely** weather." (tom plano) / "**Great**. Just **great**." Understatement britânico é dito de forma **calma e neutra** — e é isso que o torna engraçado.

- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L3/pron-01.m4a) (deadpan) Oh, lovely weather for a picnic.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L3/pron-02.m4a) (calm) Well, that could have gone better.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L3/pron-03.m4a) (flat) Great. Just great.

<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Researcher:** Laughter itself appears to be universal. Babies laugh long before they can speak, and researchers have observed play vocalizations resembling laughter in apes and other animals. But what people find funny varies enormously. British humour, for instance, is famous for understatement and irony. Saying "It's a bit damp" during a flood is, for many Britons, both funny and polite. Many Americans, by contrast, are often described as preferring a more direct, energetic style. In Brazil, humour is frequently warm and teasing, and it's often used to cope with difficult situations — there's a long tradition of making jokes about politics, inflation and everyday frustrations. Interestingly, what travels best across cultures tends to be physical comedy. Charlie Chaplin and Mr. Bean are loved almost everywhere, partly because they don't depend on words. Puns and wordplay, on the other hand, are notoriously difficult to translate. So, if you want to be funny in another language, my advice is: start with self-deprecation and situations people recognize, and save the puns for later.

## 📖 Reading — The serious science of humour [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L3/reading.m4a)

Why do we laugh? For a phenomenon so familiar, humour has proved surprisingly difficult to explain. Philosophers have offered several theories over the centuries. The oldest, associated with Plato and Thomas Hobbes, is the "superiority theory": we laugh at the misfortunes or foolishness of others because it makes us feel superior. This arguably explains slapstick and some teasing, but it hardly accounts for a clever pun. A second explanation, the "relief theory", linked to Sigmund Freud among others, suggests that laughter releases nervous energy or suppressed feelings — which may explain why people sometimes laugh at funerals or after a frightening experience. The most widely discussed today, however, is the "incongruity theory": we laugh when something violates our expectations in a way that makes sense on second thought. A punchline works because it forces us to reinterpret what came before. More recently, the researchers Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren proposed what they call the "benign violation theory". According to this idea, something is funny when it is simultaneously wrong in some way — a violation of how things should be — and yet harmless, or benign. Tickling is a good illustration: a mild physical "attack" that is clearly not dangerous. The theory also helps explain why a joke can fail in two opposite ways. If it is too safe, it is boring; if the violation feels too real or too painful, it is offensive. Needless to say, no theory captures everything. Humour depends heavily on context, culture and timing, and attempts to analyse a joke tend, ironically, to kill it. As the American writer E. B. White reportedly put it, analysing humour is like dissecting a frog: few people are interested, and the frog dies.

**Questions:**
1. What does the superiority theory claim?
2. What does the incongruity theory claim?
3. What is the benign violation theory?
4. In what two ways can a joke fail, according to this theory?

## 📝 Writing

Write an opinion column (280–320 words) titled "Is anything off-limits in comedy?". Use at least six different comment adverbs and one example of irony or understatement.

**Model:**

> Is anything off-limits in comedy?  
>   
> Few debates generate as much heat, and, ironically, as little laughter, as the question of what comedians should be allowed to joke about. Every few months, a comedian somewhere is accused of crossing a line, and the same arguments are repeated: either we are "too sensitive" or comedians are "punching down".  
>   
> Admittedly, comedy has always depended on taking risks. Many of the most celebrated comedians in history became famous precisely because they said things others didn't dare to. Satire, in particular, has long been one of the few weapons ordinary people have against the powerful. Unsurprisingly, authoritarian governments tend to have very little tolerance for jokes about themselves.  
>   
> To be fair, however, freedom to joke is not the same as freedom from criticism. If a comedian makes a joke that audiences find cruel, they are entitled to say so — and to stop buying tickets. That is not censorship; it is feedback. Frankly, the comedians who complain loudest about "cancel culture" often seem to have more media attention than ever.  
>   
> What matters, arguably, is not the topic itself but the target. Joking about a tragedy is not necessarily wrong; joking at the expense of its victims usually is. Some of the funniest comedy I've seen dealt with illness, grief and racism — but the people being laughed at were the powerful, the absurd or the comedian themselves, not those already suffering.  
>   
> Needless to say, there will never be a rulebook that everyone agrees on. Humour depends on context, timing and trust between performer and audience. A joke that works in a small comedy club may fall flat on social media, where context disappears.  
>   
> So, is anything off-limits? Perhaps not in principle. But a comedian who ignores the difference between laughing with people and laughing at them is, to put it mildly, not doing their job very well.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — Humor britânico x brasileiro

O humor britânico é famoso por **ironia, understatement e autodepreciação** (pense em *Monty Python* ou *The Office* britânico). O brasileiro tende a ser mais **caloroso, exagerado e provocador**, com forte tradição de sátira política (de Chico Anysio a grupos de humor da internet). Uma dica: quando um britânico diz **"That's very brave"** sobre sua ideia, pode ser que ele ache a ideia péssima!
