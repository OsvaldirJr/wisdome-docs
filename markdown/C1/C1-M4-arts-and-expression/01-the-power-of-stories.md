## 🧩 Grammar: Descriptive language

**1. Adjetivos compostos** (duas palavras + hífen, antes do substantivo):
- **adjetivo/advérbio + particípio:** well-known, long-awaited, badly-paid, newly-built, highly-respected
- **substantivo + particípio:** sun-drenched, rain-soaked, home-made, hand-written, tear-stained
- **adjetivo + substantivo-ed:** blue-eyed, broken-hearted, open-minded, short-tempered, bad-tempered
- **substantivo/adjetivo + -ing:** heart-warming, record-breaking, mouth-watering, good-looking, never-ending
- **número + substantivo (singular!):** a **ten-minute** walk, a **five-star** hotel, a **two-hour** film

**2. Verbos expressivos** (mostram **como**):
- **walk:** stroll (sem pressa), stride (confiante), stagger (cambaleando), tiptoe (na ponta dos pés), wander (sem rumo), limp (mancando)
- **say:** whisper, mutter, mumble, yell, snap (irritado), sigh, stammer
- **look:** glance, stare, gaze (com admiração), peer (com dificuldade), glimpse
- **laugh:** giggle, chuckle, burst out laughing · **cry:** sob, weep

**3. Ordem dos adjetivos** (nativos seguem intuitivamente):
**opinião → tamanho → idade → forma → cor → origem → material → propósito + substantivo**
"a **lovely little old** **white** **Portuguese** **stone** cottage"
Na prática, use no máximo 2–3 adjetivos seguidos.

**4. "Show, don't tell"** — em vez de **dizer** a emoção, **mostre** com ações e detalhes:
- Tell: "She was nervous."
- Show: "She **glanced** at the clock for the third time, **twisting** her ring around her finger."

| Tipo | Exemplos |
|---|---|
| adv + particípio | well-known, long-awaited, highly-paid |
| substantivo + particípio | sun-drenched, home-made, rain-soaked |
| adj + substantivo-ed | blue-eyed, open-minded, kind-hearted |
| ... + -ing | heart-breaking, mouth-watering, record-breaking |
| número + substantivo | a three-hour flight, a ten-year-old boy |
| Ordem | opinião-tamanho-idade-forma-cor-origem-material-propósito |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L1/example-01.m4a) The long-awaited sequel turned out to be a gripping, beautifully-written novel. — _A tão aguardada sequência acabou sendo um romance envolvente e lindamente escrito._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L1/example-02.m4a) He staggered into the kitchen, rain-soaked and exhausted, and muttered an apology. — _Ele entrou cambaleando na cozinha, encharcado e exausto, e resmungou um pedido de desculpas._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L1/example-03.m4a) We rented a charming little whitewashed fisherman's cottage near the beach. — _Alugamos uma charmosa casinha caiada de pescador perto da praia._

> 💡 **Dica:** Erro clássico: "a ten-**minutes** walk". Em adjetivos compostos com número, o substantivo fica **no singular**: a ten-**minute** walk, a five-**year**-old girl, a two-**hour** meeting.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ It's a five-minutes walk from here.  
  ✅ **It's a five-minute walk from here.**  
  Adjetivo composto com número: substantivo no singular.
- ❌ a wooden old beautiful house  
  ✅ **a beautiful old wooden house**  
  Ordem: opinião → idade → material.
- ❌ She was very very sad and she cried a lot.  
  ✅ **Her shoulders shook as she sobbed quietly into her hands.**  
  Show, don't tell: verbos precisos em vez de intensificadores repetidos.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Acento em adjetivos compostos

Em geral, adjetivos compostos têm acento **nas duas partes**, com o mais forte na **segunda**: well-**KNOWN**, long-a**WAI**ted, home-**MADE**. Mas antes de um substantivo, o acento pode mudar para a primeira parte: a **WELL**-known **AC**tor.

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Speaker:** Why do stories grip us so powerfully? Part of the answer may lie in the brain. When we read a list of facts, the areas involved in processing language become active. But a number of studies have suggested that vivid descriptions can engage other areas too. In one experiment, words with strong smell associations, like "coffee" or "perfume", appeared to activate regions linked to the sense of smell. Metaphors involving texture — "a rough day", "a velvety voice" — seemed to engage areas related to touch. Researchers are cautious about these findings, and brain-imaging studies are notoriously difficult to interpret. But they're consistent with something writers have always known: a well-told story isn't just understood, it's experienced. That's also why stories are remembered so much better than statistics. Tell people that thousands of children are hungry, and they nod. Tell them about one child — her name, her face, what she had for breakfast — and they act.

## 📖 Reading — Six words [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L1/reading.m4a)

"For sale: baby shoes, never worn." These six words are often described as the shortest story ever written, and they are frequently attributed to Ernest Hemingway, who is said to have written them to win a bet. There is, however, no reliable evidence that he did; similar newspaper adverts had appeared years before. Whoever wrote it, the story is a perfect illustration of a principle at the heart of good writing: what is left out can be as powerful as what is put in. The reader is never told what happened. There is no description of grief, no explanation, no ending. And yet most readers feel a sudden, heart-breaking weight. The story works precisely because the reader's imagination does the work. Hemingway himself described a similar idea, sometimes called the "iceberg theory": a writer who knows his subject well can omit much of it, and the reader will still sense what lies beneath the surface. Many writing teachers express the same idea with the phrase "show, don't tell". Rather than stating that a character is lonely, a writer might describe him setting the table for two out of habit, then slowly putting one plate away. Rather than declaring that a city is poor, she might describe children playing football with a ball made of plastic bags. Of course, the rule can be overused. Sometimes the clearest, most efficient choice is simply to tell. But the six-word story reminds us of something worth remembering in any kind of writing, from novels to emails: readers are not passive recipients of information. Given the right details, they will build the world themselves.

**Questions:**
1. Is there evidence that Hemingway wrote the six-word story?
2. Why does the story work?
3. What is the "iceberg theory"?
4. Give one example of "showing" from the text.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — Contação de histórias

Do **cordel** nordestino às histórias de Monteiro Lobato, o Brasil tem uma rica tradição oral e escrita de narrativas. Em inglês, **storytelling** virou palavra-chave em marketing, liderança e educação. Em muitas universidades americanas, cursos de **creative writing** são populares, e o conselho mais repetido é exatamente o desta aula: **"Show, don't tell."**
