## 🔥 Warm-up
"Não é que eu não goste de arte moderna — é que eu não entendo." "Tudo o que o artista fez foi colocar uma banana na parede." Essas frases usam **cleft sentences**: estruturas que **destacam** a parte mais importante. No B2 você viu o básico; agora vem a versão completa. Tema: **arte e valor**.

- Have you ever looked at a work of art and thought "I could have done that"?
- Should public money be spent on art? Why / why not?

## 🧩 Grammar: Advanced cleft sentences

Cleft sentences dividem uma frase em duas partes para **destacar** uma informação. Revisão: "**What** I need **is** a break." / "**It was** Ana **who** called."

**1. What-cleft com ações (do / happen):**
- "**What she did was** (to) leave the room." (destaca a ação)
- "**What happened was (that)** the alarm went off."
- "**What I'd do is** call the gallery directly."

**2. All-cleft** (= a única coisa, só):
- "**All I want is** an honest opinion." / "**All he did was** sign his name."
- "**All** you need to do **is** ask."

**3. Substantivos gerais + cleft:**
- **The thing (that)... is**: "**The thing that** annoys me **is** the price."
- **The reason (why)... is that**: "**The reason** it's famous **is that** it was stolen."
- **The person who / The place where / The day when / The way**: "**The place where** you really see her talent **is** in her drawings."
- **Where / When / Why / How** sem antecedente: "**Where** the film works **is** in its silences." / "**How** he did it **is** a mystery."

**4. It's not that... it's (just) that...** (corrigir um mal-entendido):
- "**It's not that** I hate modern art; **it's just that** some of it feels empty."
- "**It isn't** the price **that** bothers me; **it's** the attitude."

**5. Cleft reverso** (a informação destacada vem primeiro):
- "A good night's sleep **is what** I need." / "That**'s why** I left." / "That**'s where** we disagree."

**Fala informal:** "**The thing is**, ..." / "**What happened was**, ..." são muito comuns como **marcadores de discurso**.

| Estrutura | Exemplo |
|---|---|
| What + S + did was (to) + verbo | What he did was paint over it. |
| What happened was (that)... | What happened was that the painting fell. |
| All + S + V + is/was... | All I did was ask a question. |
| The thing / reason / place... | The reason I love it is that it's honest. |
| Where / How... is | Where it fails is in the ending. |
| It's not that... it's that... | It's not that it's bad; it's that it's boring. |
| Cleft reverso | That's exactly what I mean. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L4/example-01.m4a) What Banksy did was shred his own painting moments after it had been sold at auction. — _O que Banksy fez foi triturar a própria pintura momentos depois de ela ter sido vendida em leilão._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L4/example-02.m4a) It's not that contemporary art is meaningless; it's that it often asks the viewer to do some of the work. — _Não é que a arte contemporânea não tenha sentido; é que ela muitas vezes pede que o espectador faça parte do trabalho._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L4/example-03.m4a) The reason Inhotim is so special is that art and nature are completely intertwined. — _A razão pela qual Inhotim é tão especial é que arte e natureza estão completamente entrelaçadas._

> 💡 **Dica:** "**O que eu fiz foi...**" → "**What I did was** ..." Note que, com **do**, o verbo seguinte pode vir com ou sem **to**: "What I did was (to) call her" — sem to é mais comum na fala.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ What I did it was call the police.  
  ✅ **What I did was call the police.**  
  Não repita o objeto (it) na what-cleft.
- ❌ All what I want is some peace.  
  ✅ **All I want is some peace.**  
  All + oração (sem what).
- ❌ It's not that I don't like it, is that I don't have time.  
  ✅ **It's not that I don't like it; it's that I don't have time.**  
  Repita o it's na segunda parte.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Ênfase em cleft sentences

Na cleft sentence, a **informação destacada** (depois de is/was) recebe o acento mais forte, e há uma pequena pausa antes dela: "What I NEED | is a BREAK." / "It's not that I HATE it; | it's that I don't UNderstand it."

- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L4/pron-01.m4a) What she DID | was LEAVE.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L4/pron-02.m4a) ALL I want | is an HONest opinion.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L4/pron-03.m4a) It's not that I HATE it; | it's that I don't UNderstand it.

## 🎧 Listening — The painting that shredded itself

_Uma repórter relata um dos episódios mais famosos do mercado de arte recente._

**Listen first, without reading.** [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L4/listening-full.m4a)

**Questions:**
1. What happened just after the painting was sold in 2018?
2. Why had Banksy built the device, according to his video?
3. What was the work renamed, and what did it sell for in 2021?
4. What is the reporter's final point?

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Reporter:** In October 2018, at an auction at Sotheby's in London, a framed copy of Banksy's Girl with Balloon was sold for just over one million pounds. What happened next stunned the room. Moments after the hammer came down, an alarm sounded and the canvas began to slide through a shredder hidden inside the frame. Halfway through, the shredder stopped. Banksy later posted a video suggesting that he had built the device years earlier, in case the work was ever put up for auction. Many people assumed the stunt would destroy the painting's value. What it actually did was the opposite. The half-shredded work was renamed Love is in the Bin, and in 2021 it was sold again — this time for more than eighteen million pounds. The reason it became so valuable is that the shredding itself became part of the artwork, and, arguably, part of art history. It's not that the art market missed the joke; it's that the market turned the joke into a product.

## 📖 Reading — What is art worth? [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M4-L4/reading.m4a)

In 2019, at the Art Basel fair in Miami, the Italian artist Maurizio Cattelan exhibited a work called Comedian: a real banana, attached to a wall with grey duct tape. Editions of the work were reportedly sold for around 120,000 dollars each. Then, to the delight of photographers, a performance artist peeled the banana off the wall and ate it. The gallery was unconcerned. What the buyers had purchased, it explained, was not the fruit itself but a certificate of authenticity and instructions for replacing it. The episode provoked a familiar mixture of laughter and outrage. For many people, it confirmed their suspicion that contemporary art is a joke played on the rich. Yet the story raises a serious question: where does the value of art actually lie? It is not that the materials of most great art are especially precious; paint, canvas and stone are relatively cheap. What we pay for, arguably, is something less tangible: an idea, a reputation, a story, a moment in history. Vincent van Gogh is often said to have sold only one painting in his lifetime — an oversimplification, but one that captures how little his work was valued while he lived. Today, his paintings rank among the most expensive ever sold. Nothing about the canvases changed. What changed was how people saw them. None of this means that every expensive work is a masterpiece, or that every unknown artist is a misunderstood genius. Markets can be driven by fashion, speculation and the desire for status. But perhaps the banana on the wall was doing exactly what art has always done: making us look again at something ordinary and ask what it means. The thing is, whether that is worth 120,000 dollars is a question no theory can answer for us.

**Questions:**
1. What did the buyers of Comedian actually purchase?
2. What, according to the writer, do we really pay for in art?
3. What point does the van Gogh example make?
4. What might the banana have been doing, according to the writer?

## 📝 Writing

Write an article (280–320 words) for a cultural website titled "Why I changed my mind about..." (a type of art, music, a film genre or an artist). Use at least six advanced cleft sentences.

**Model:**

> Why I changed my mind about contemporary art  
>   
> For years, my opinion of contemporary art could be summed up in one sentence: "My nephew could have done that." It's not that I disliked art in general — I loved Renaissance paintings and the bright, bold canvases of Tarsila do Amaral. It's just that I couldn't see the point of empty rooms, piles of bricks or a single line on a white wall.  
>   
> What changed my mind was a weekend at Inhotim, the enormous art park in Minas Gerais, a few years ago. I went reluctantly, mainly because a friend had already bought the tickets.  
>   
> The first surprise was the setting. Instead of white walls and silent guards, what I found were tropical gardens, lakes and pavilions hidden among the trees. The thing that struck me most, though, was how differently I felt when I wasn't being told what to think.  
>   
> One installation in particular stayed with me. All it consisted of was a dark room and a sound recording of the earth itself. I had no idea what I was supposed to understand. What I actually did was sit there for twenty minutes, completely absorbed, feeling something I couldn't name.  
>   
> That's when I realized what I'd been getting wrong. I'd been treating contemporary art like a puzzle with a correct answer, and getting annoyed when I couldn't solve it. Where it really works, I think, is in creating an experience rather than delivering a message.  
>   
> I still don't like everything, of course. Some works seem to me pretentious, and the prices at international auctions are, frankly, absurd. But the reason I keep going back to galleries now is that I've stopped asking "What does it mean?" and started asking "What does it do to me?"  
>   
> My nephew, incidentally, is now studying fine art. Perhaps he could have done it after all.
