Um jornal sério nunca diz "O político roubou dinheiro" antes de uma condenação. Diz: "The politician **allegedly** stole..." ou "He **is alleged to have** stolen...". Essa linguagem de **distanciamento** protege o autor e mostra ao leitor o **grau de certeza**. Tema: **desinformação e confiança**.

- Have you ever shared something online that turned out to be false?
- Which sources of news do you trust most? Why?

## 🧩 Grammar: Distancing language

**1. Advérbios de distanciamento** — mostram que a informação **não foi confirmada** pelo autor:
- **allegedly** (acusação ainda não provada): "The mayor **allegedly** used public funds."
- **reportedly** (segundo relatos/a imprensa): "The singer is **reportedly** planning a tour."
- **supposedly** (supostamente — com dúvida/ironia): "The diet **supposedly** cures everything."
- **apparently** (ao que parece), **purportedly** (formal, = supostamente), **ostensibly** (aparentemente, mas na verdade não)

**2. Passivas de relato** (revisão B2 + todos os tempos):
- **It is + particípio + that**: "**It is alleged that** he lied." / "**It has been reported that**..."
- **Sujeito + be + particípio + infinitivo**:
  • presente: "He **is said to be** in Portugal."
  • contínuo: "He **is thought to be hiding** abroad."
  • passado: "He **is alleged to have lied**."
  • passado passivo: "The data **are believed to have been manipulated**."
  • passado do relato: "She **was reported to have left** the country."
- **There is said / thought to be**: "**There are thought to be** thousands of fake accounts."

**3. Reporting verbs que mostram atitude:**
- **Neutro:** say, state, report, explain
- **Dúvida do autor:** **claim** (afirmar, sem prova), **allege**, **purport**
- **Admitir:** **admit**, **concede** (admitir a contragosto), **acknowledge**
- **Negar:** **deny** (+ -ing / that), **reject**, **dismiss** (descartar como sem importância)
- **Insistir:** **insist**, **maintain** (sustentar), **assert**
- **Prometer/ameaçar:** pledge, vow, threaten

Compare: "The CEO **said** profits were stable" (neutro) x "The CEO **claimed** profits were stable" (o jornalista duvida) x "The CEO **conceded** profits had fallen" (admitiu a contragosto).

| Estrutura | Exemplo |
|---|---|
| allegedly / reportedly | He allegedly hacked the system. |
| It is + pp + that | It is claimed that the photo is fake. |
| S + is + pp + to + verbo | She is said to be very ill. |
| S + is + pp + to have + pp | He is believed to have fled. |
| ... + to have been + pp | The votes are alleged to have been altered. |
| There is thought to be | There are thought to be 200 victims. |
| claim / concede / deny / insist | He denied having seen the email. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M5-L3/example-01.m4a) The video, which is reported to have been viewed millions of times, was later shown to be a deepfake. — _O vídeo, que teria sido visto milhões de vezes, depois se mostrou um deepfake._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M5-L3/example-02.m4a) The company has repeatedly denied using customers' data without consent, but it conceded that its privacy policy had been unclear. — _A empresa negou repetidamente usar dados de clientes sem consentimento, mas admitiu que sua política de privacidade era pouco clara._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M5-L3/example-03.m4a) There are thought to be thousands of automated accounts spreading political content. — _Acredita-se que existam milhares de contas automatizadas espalhando conteúdo político._

> 💡 **Dica:** **Claim** em inglês carrega **dúvida**: "He **claims** to be a doctor" sugere que talvez não seja. Para relatar de forma neutra, use **say** ou **state**. Escolher o verbo certo é escolher sua **atitude** em relação à informação.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ He is said that he is rich.  
  ✅ **He is said to be rich. / It is said that he is rich.**  
  Duas estruturas possíveis; não misture.
- ❌ She is believed to leave the country last week.  
  ✅ **She is believed to have left the country last week.**  
  Ação anterior = to have + particípio.
- ❌ He denied to have taken the money.  
  ✅ **He denied taking / having taken the money. / He denied that he had taken...**  
  deny + -ing ou that.

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Researcher:** In 2018, a team of researchers at MIT published a study in the journal Science that analysed around 126,000 stories shared on Twitter between 2006 and 2017. Each story had been checked by independent fact-checking organizations. The findings were striking. False stories were found to spread significantly further, faster and more widely than true ones. According to the study, false news was about 70 percent more likely to be retweeted than the truth. Why? The researchers suggested that false stories tend to be more novel and surprising, and that they provoke stronger emotional reactions, such as disgust and fear. Interestingly, the study concluded that it was humans, not automated bots, who were primarily responsible for the difference. Bots were found to spread true and false stories at similar rates. The implication is uncomfortable. If we want to fight misinformation, it is not enough to blame algorithms or foreign agents. We also have to look at our own behaviour — and pause before we click "share".

</details>

## 📖 Reading — The age of deepfakes [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M5-L3/reading.m4a)

In 2018, a video appeared online in which former US president Barack Obama seemed to insult his successor. The video was not real. It had been created by the media company BuzzFeed, in collaboration with the filmmaker Jordan Peele, who voiced the words, to warn the public about a new kind of technology: deepfakes. Since then, the tools needed to produce convincing fake video and audio have become dramatically cheaper and easier to use. What once required a professional studio can now reportedly be done in minutes with a laptop or even a phone. The consequences are already visible. Fake audio recordings of politicians have allegedly been circulated shortly before elections in several countries, when there is little time to debunk them. Criminals are reported to have used cloned voices of company executives to persuade employees to transfer money. Ordinary people, too, have been targeted with fabricated images. Perhaps the most worrying effect, however, is not that people will believe fake content but that they will stop believing real content. Researchers have called this the "liar's dividend": when anything could be fake, those caught on camera doing something wrong can simply claim that the evidence has been fabricated. Truth itself becomes a matter of opinion. There are responses. Technology companies are said to be developing tools to detect manipulated media and to label AI-generated content, and several governments have introduced or proposed regulations. Journalists and fact-checkers are increasingly trained in verification techniques. But most experts concede that technology alone cannot solve the problem. In the end, the best defence may be an old-fashioned one: a healthy scepticism, combined with trust in reliable, accountable sources.

**Questions:**
1. Why was the fake Obama video created?
2. How have criminals reportedly used deepfakes?
3. What is the "liar's dividend"?
4. What may be the best defence, according to the text?
