## 🧩 Grammar: Describing data and academic hedging

**1. Descrevendo tendências** — duas estruturas equivalentes:
- **Verbo + advérbio:** "Sales **rose sharply**." / "Prices **fell slightly**."
- **Adjetivo + substantivo:** "There was **a sharp rise** in sales." / "**a slight fall** in prices."

**Verbos:** rise, increase, grow, climb, **soar**, **rocket** ↗ · fall, decrease, drop, decline, **plummet**, **plunge** ↘ · **fluctuate** ↕ · **level off**, **stabilize**, remain steady → · **peak**, reach a high/low
**Advérbios/adjetivos de intensidade:** dramatically, sharply, significantly, considerably, steadily, gradually, slightly, marginally

**Preposições** (erro comum!):
- rose **from** 10% **to** 25% · rose **by** 15 percentage points · **a** rise **in** prices · **a** rise **of** 15% · peaked **at** 30%

**Multiplicadores:** doubled / tripled / a **threefold** increase / **twice as many as** / **three times as high as**

**2. Aproximações:**
- **just over / just under / roughly / approximately / around / nearly / almost**
- **the vast majority / a significant minority / a small proportion / one in five / a quarter**

**3. Hedging acadêmico** (suavizar quando a evidência é limitada):
- Verbos: **appear to, seem to, tend to, suggest, indicate**
- Modais: **may, might, could**
- Expressões: **it would appear that / there is evidence to suggest that / to some extent / in some cases / it is likely that**
- Advérbios: **possibly, probably, arguably, apparently**

Compare:
- Forte demais: "Social media **causes** depression."
- Acadêmico: "There **is some evidence to suggest** that heavy social media use **may be associated with** higher rates of depression."

**Boosters** (quando a evidência é forte): clearly, undoubtedly, it is evident that, the data **demonstrate**.

| Movimento | Verbo + advérbio | Adjetivo + substantivo |
|---|---|---|
| ↗ muito | soared / rose sharply | a dramatic / sharp rise |
| ↗ pouco | rose slightly | a slight / marginal increase |
| ↘ muito | plummeted / fell dramatically | a steep / sharp fall |
| ↕ | fluctuated | fluctuations |
| → | levelled off / remained stable | a period of stability |
| topo | peaked at 40% | reached a peak of 40% |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M3-L3/example-01.m4a) The number of internet users in Brazil rose steadily throughout the 2010s, reaching the vast majority of the population by the end of the decade. — _O número de usuários de internet no Brasil cresceu de forma constante nos anos 2010, alcançando a grande maioria da população no fim da década._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M3-L3/example-02.m4a) There appears to be a correlation between hours of sleep and exam performance, although further research would be needed to establish causation. — _Parece haver uma correlação entre horas de sono e desempenho em provas, embora fossem necessárias mais pesquisas para estabelecer causalidade._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M3-L3/example-03.m4a) Just under a third of respondents said they had never used a public library. — _Pouco menos de um terço dos entrevistados disse nunca ter usado uma biblioteca pública._

> 💡 **Dica:** Atenção à diferença: **percent** (%) x **percentage points**. Se a taxa de desemprego vai de 10% para 12%, ela subiu **2 percentage points** — mas aumentou **20 percent** em termos relativos. Jornalistas confundem isso o tempo todo!

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ There was a sharp rise of sales.  
  ✅ **There was a sharp rise in sales.**  
  a rise IN + o que subiu; a rise OF + quantidade (a rise of 10%).
- ❌ The data proves that coffee causes cancer.  
  ✅ **The data suggest that coffee may be associated with...**  
  Use hedging quando há só correlação. (Em estilo acadêmico, data costuma ser plural.)
- ❌ Prices increased in 5% to 10%.  
  ✅ **Prices increased from 5% to 10%. / increased by 5 percentage points.**  
  from... to / by (diferença).

This figure remained fairly stable until 2014, when the city opened its first network of cycle lanes. ## 📖 Reading — How to lie with statistics [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M3-L3/reading.m4a)

In 1954, the American writer Darrell Huff published a small book with a provocative title: How to Lie with Statistics. Despite its age, many of its warnings remain remarkably relevant. Perhaps the most common trap is confusing correlation with causation. Countries that consume more chocolate per person, for example, tend to have won more Nobel Prizes per capita — a correlation highlighted, half-jokingly, in a 2012 article in a medical journal. Few would seriously conclude that chocolate makes people smarter. A far more likely explanation is that both are associated with a third factor: national wealth. Yet similar reasoning appears regularly in headlines about diet, technology and health. A second trap involves relative and absolute risk. A headline announcing that a medicine "doubles the risk" of a side effect sounds alarming. But if the risk rises from one in 10,000 to two in 10,000, the absolute increase is tiny. Both statements are technically true; only one gives the reader a fair picture. Third, there is the problem of the sample. A survey of 200 enthusiastic app users cannot tell us much about the population as a whole, however impressive its results may appear. Finally, graphs themselves can mislead. By starting the vertical axis at a number other than zero, a designer can make a marginal change look dramatic. None of this means that statistics are useless — quite the opposite. Used carefully, they are among the most powerful tools we have for understanding the world. The point is that numbers, like words, need to be read critically. As a rule of thumb, it would seem wise to ask three questions of any statistic: compared to what, measured how, and according to whom?

**Questions:**
1. What is the chocolate example meant to show?
2. What is the difference between relative and absolute risk in the example?
3. How can graphs mislead?
4. What three questions does the writer suggest asking?

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — "Lies, damned lies, and statistics"

A frase **"There are three kinds of lies: lies, damned lies, and statistics"** foi popularizada por Mark Twain, que a atribuiu ao primeiro-ministro britânico Benjamin Disraeli — mas não há registro de Disraeli tê-la dito. Ironicamente, a origem dessa frase sobre dados enganosos é, ela mesma, incerta!

## ✅ Resumo
- rose sharply = a sharp rise IN; from X to Y; by X points; peaked AT.
- Multiplicadores: doubled, a threefold increase, three times as high as.
- Aproximações: just over / under, roughly, the vast majority.
- Hedging: appear to, the data suggest, may be associated with. Correlação ≠ causalidade.
