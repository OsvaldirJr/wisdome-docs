## 🔥 Warm-up
Num debate de alto nível, quem convence não é quem ignora o outro lado — é quem o **reconhece** e depois o **supera**: "**Admittedly**, the plan is expensive. **However much** it costs, though, ..." Esse é o **movimento concessivo**, marca do pensamento crítico. Tema: **dilemas éticos**.

- Is it ever acceptable to lie? Give an example.
- Would you sacrifice one person to save five? Why / why not?

## 🧩 Grammar: Advanced concession

**1. However / Whatever / Whoever / Wherever / Whenever + oração** (= não importa como/o que/quem...)
- **However + adjetivo/advérbio + S + V**: "**However hard** I try, I can't understand it." / "**However much** it costs, we'll pay."
- "**Whatever** you decide, I'll support you." / "**Whoever** wins, the country will be divided."
- Com **may** (mais formal): "**However** difficult it **may** be, ..."

**2. No matter + what / how / who / where / when**
- "**No matter how** many times I explain, he doesn't listen." (≈ However many times...)
- "**No matter what** happens, stay calm."

**3. Adjetivo / advérbio + though / as + S + V** (formal, enfático):
- "**Difficult though** it is, we must decide." / "**Tempting as** it may be, we should not lie."

**4. Concessão com advérbios e expressões** (reconhecer → contrapor):
- **Admittedly**, ... / **Granted**, ... / **It is true that** ..., **but** ...
- **While it is true that** ..., ... / **Although it may be argued that** ..., ...
- **Be that as it may**, ... / **Even so**, ... / **All the same**, ... / **That said**, ...
- "**Admittedly**, lying can sometimes protect people. **Even so**, I believe honesty is the better policy in the long run."

**5. Much as / However much** (revisão da M1-L3): "**Much as** I admire her, I can't agree."

**Estrutura de argumento concessivo:**
**reconhecer** (Admittedly / Granted / While it is true that...) → **contrapor** (However / Even so / Be that as it may) → **reforçar a sua posição**.

| Estrutura | Exemplo |
|---|---|
| However + adj + S + V | However rich you are, you can't buy time. |
| Whatever / Whoever / Wherever | Whatever happens, call me. |
| No matter how / what | No matter what they say, I'm going. |
| Adj + though/as + S + V | Strange though it sounds, it's true. |
| Admittedly / Granted, ... Even so, ... | Granted, it's risky. Even so, it's worth it. |
| While it is true that..., ... | While it is true that it's costly, it saves lives. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M3-L4/example-01.m4a) However carefully self-driving cars are programmed, they will occasionally face situations in which every option causes harm. — _Por mais cuidadosamente que carros autônomos sejam programados, ocasionalmente enfrentarão situações em que toda opção causa dano._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M3-L4/example-02.m4a) Admittedly, animal testing has contributed to many medical advances. Even so, alternatives should be used wherever possible. — _É verdade que os testes em animais contribuíram para muitos avanços médicos. Mesmo assim, alternativas devem ser usadas sempre que possível._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M3-L4/example-03.m4a) Tempting though it may be to blame individuals, the problem is largely structural. — _Por mais tentador que seja culpar indivíduos, o problema é em grande parte estrutural._

> 💡 **Dica:** "**Por mais que**" tem várias traduções elegantes: **However much** I love it... / **Much as** I love it... / **No matter how much** I love it... / **Hard as** I tried... (para esforço). Varie para mostrar repertório C1!

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ However I try hard, I can't do it.  
  ✅ **However hard I try, I can't do it.**  
  However + adjetivo/advérbio juntos, depois sujeito + verbo.
- ❌ No matter what happen, call me.  
  ✅ **No matter what happens, call me.**  
  O verbo da oração concorda normalmente (happens).
- ❌ Difficult though it may is, we must try.  
  ✅ **Difficult though it may be, we must try.**  
  may + verbo base (be).

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Entonação concessiva

No movimento concessivo, a primeira parte (o reconhecimento) tem entonação **ascendente/suspensa**, preparando o contraste; a segunda (sua posição) tem entonação **descendente e firme**: "Admittedly, it's expensive ↗... | Even so, it's worth it ↘."

- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M3-L4/pron-01.m4a) Admittedly, it's expensive ↗... | Even so, it's worth it ↘.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M3-L4/pron-02.m4a) However hard I try ↗, | I can't agree ↘.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M3-L4/pron-03.m4a) Granted, it's risky ↗. | But we have no choice ↘.

**Researcher:** In 2016, researchers at MIT launched an online experiment called the Moral Machine. It presented visitors with variations of the famous trolley problem, adapted for self-driving cars: should the car save its passengers or the pedestrians? The young or the elderly? More lives or fewer? Millions of people from over two hundred countries and territories took part, and the results were published in Nature in 2018. Some preferences were broadly shared: in general, people chose to spare more lives rather than fewer, humans rather than animals, and the young rather than the old. But there were also notable cultural differences. Participants from some regions, for example, showed a weaker preference for sparing younger people. Whatever you think of the method — and it has been criticized for oversimplifying real situations — the study raises a difficult question. However universal our moral instincts may seem, they may not be the same everywhere. So whose values should a machine follow?

## 📖 Reading — Is it ever right to lie? [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M3-L4/reading.m4a)

Few moral questions are as old, or as practical, as this one. The eighteenth-century philosopher Immanuel Kant took an uncompromising position. Lying, he argued, is always wrong, whatever the consequences — even, in a much-debated example, when a murderer comes to your door asking where your friend is hiding. However extreme this may sound, Kant's reasoning was that a lie treats other people as mere instruments and undermines the trust on which all communication depends. Most people find this conclusion hard to accept. Admittedly, honesty is a value almost every culture shares. Even so, few of us believe we should tell a friend that their wedding speech was boring, or tell a terrified patient every grim statistic at once. Consequentialist philosophers, following thinkers such as John Stuart Mill, argue that the morality of an action depends on its outcomes. If a lie prevents serious harm, it may be not only permissible but right. Yet this view has its own difficulties. Who decides which consequences matter, and how far ahead must we look? A "white lie" that spares someone's feelings today may damage their trust tomorrow. Psychologists add an interesting twist. Research suggests that most of us lie more often than we think, and that we tend to judge our own lies far more generously than those of others. Much as we may admire honesty in principle, our everyday behaviour is more complicated. Perhaps the most realistic answer is that honesty is a strong default, not an absolute rule. Granted, that leaves us with grey areas. But moral life, whatever philosophers may wish, has always been full of them.

**Questions:**
1. What was Kant's position on lying?
2. Why did Kant believe this?
3. What do consequentialists argue?
4. What does research suggest about how we judge lies?

## 📝 Writing

Write a discursive essay (280–320 words): "Is it ever right to break the law?" Present both sides, use at least five concessive structures and reach a clear conclusion.

**Model:**

> Is it ever right to break the law?  
>   
> In a democratic society, laws are meant to reflect shared values and protect citizens. It might therefore seem obvious that they should always be obeyed. However, history offers numerous examples of people who broke the law and are now remembered as heroes. This essay will consider whether breaking the law can ever be justified.  
>   
> There are strong arguments for obeying the law in all circumstances. If individuals could ignore any law they disagreed with, the result would be chaos. Whatever our personal views, the rule of law protects the weak from the strong and allows disagreements to be resolved peacefully. Admittedly, laws are sometimes unjust. Even so, democratic societies offer legal ways of changing them, such as elections, protests and the courts.  
>   
> On the other hand, it is difficult to argue that every law deserves obedience. Rosa Parks broke the law when she refused to give up her bus seat in 1955, yet few people today would say she was wrong. However legitimate a government may appear, its laws can still be deeply immoral, and legal routes to change can be slow or blocked entirely. In such cases, peaceful civil disobedience may be the only way to draw attention to injustice.  
>   
> Granted, the line between principled disobedience and simple law-breaking is not always clear. Much as we may admire activists, we should be wary of anyone who claims the right to decide which laws apply to them.  
>   
> In conclusion, I believe that breaking the law can be justified, but only in exceptional circumstances: when the law is clearly unjust, when legal options have been exhausted, and when the act is peaceful and open, with the person willing to accept the consequences. Difficult though it may be to define these limits precisely, they distinguish conscience from mere convenience.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — O "trolley problem"

O famoso **trolley problem** (problema do bonde) foi formulado pela filósofa britânica **Philippa Foot** em 1967 e popularizado depois por **Judith Jarvis Thomson**. Virou meme na internet e ganhou nova relevância com os carros autônomos. Em inglês, dizer que algo é "a real trolley problem" significa que todas as opções têm um custo moral.
