## 🧩 Grammar: Rhetorical devices and cleft sentences

**1. Regra de três (tricolon)** — três elementos paralelos soam completos e memoráveis:
- "Our app is **faster, cheaper and safer**."
- "**We listened, we learned, and we built** something better."

**2. Perguntas retóricas** — envolvem o público (você responde logo em seguida):
- "**How many of you** have waited an hour for a doctor's appointment? **Exactly.**"
- "**So, why does this matter?** Because..."

**3. Contraste e antítese:**
- "It's **not about** working harder; **it's about** working smarter."
- "**Yesterday**, it took three days. **Today**, it takes three minutes."

**4. Repetição (anáfora)** — repetir o início das frases:
- "**Every day**, families lose time. **Every day**, businesses lose money. **Every day**, we wait."

**5. Cleft sentences para foco** (revisão e expansão do B2):
- **What** + oração + **is/was**: "**What we need is** a simpler solution." / "**What surprised us was** the demand."
- **All** + oração (= a única coisa): "**All you need is** a phone."
- **The reason (why)... is that**: "**The reason why** we're different **is that** we listen."
- **The thing / The problem / The truth is (that)**: "**The problem is that** nobody trusts the current system."
- **It is/was... that/who**: "**It was** our customers **who** gave us this idea."

**6. Signposting** — sinaliza a estrutura:
- Começo: "I'd like to start by... / Let me begin with a question."
- Transição: "Let's move on to... / This brings me to... / Turning now to..."
- Ênfase: "I'd like to stress that... / The key point here is..."
- Fim: "To wrap up... / Let me leave you with one thought..."

| Recurso | Exemplo |
|---|---|
| Regra de três | Simple, fast and affordable. |
| Pergunta retórica | Sound familiar? I thought so. |
| Contraste | Not a product, but a movement. |
| Repetição | We'll grow. We'll learn. We'll lead. |
| What-cleft | What makes us different is our community. |
| It-cleft | It's the data that tells the story. |
| Signposting | This brings me to my final point. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L4/example-01.m4a) What small farmers need isn't charity; it's access to fair markets. — _Do que os pequenos agricultores precisam não é caridade; é acesso a mercados justos._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L4/example-02.m4a) How much time do you spend looking for parking every week? For most city drivers, it's hours. — _Quanto tempo você passa procurando vaga toda semana? Para a maioria dos motoristas urbanos, são horas._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L4/example-03.m4a) It was our first hundred users who showed us what the product should really be. — _Foram nossos primeiros cem usuários que nos mostraram o que o produto realmente deveria ser._

> 💡 **Dica:** Não exagere! Um pitch com perguntas retóricas a cada frase soa artificial. Uma boa receita: **uma pergunta para abrir**, **uma regra de três** para a proposta de valor e **uma cleft sentence** para o ponto principal.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ What we need it is more time.  
  ✅ **What we need is more time.**  
  Na what-cleft, não repita o sujeito (it).
- ❌ The reason why we're different is because we listen.  
  ✅ **The reason why we're different is that we listen.**  
  Em textos formais, prefira the reason... is that ("because" é redundante).
- ❌ It was our customers which gave us the idea.  
  ✅ **It was our customers who gave us the idea.**  
  Pessoas: who (ou that).

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Ritmo e pausas em apresentações

Na **regra de três**, a entonação sobe nos dois primeiros itens e **cai** no último: "faster ↗, cheaper ↗ and safer ↘". Depois de uma **pergunta retórica**, faça uma **pausa de 1–2 segundos** — o silêncio cria impacto. Em **cleft sentences**, enfatize o elemento focado.

- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L4/pron-01.m4a) Faster ↗, cheaper ↗ and safer ↘.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L4/pron-02.m4a) How many of you have waited an hour for a doctor? | (pause) | Exactly.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L4/pron-03.m4a) What we NEED | is a SIMPLER solution.

## 🎧 Listening — Three tricks from the ancient world

_Um professor de oratória explica recursos retóricos clássicos._

**Listen first, without reading.** [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L4/listening-full.m4a)

**Questions:**
1. What are Aristotle's three pillars of persuasion?
2. Why does the rule of three work?
3. What is antithesis?
4. What do the three techniques have in common?

<details>
<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Speaker:** More than two thousand years ago, Aristotle argued that persuasion rests on three pillars: ethos, your credibility; pathos, the emotions you create; and logos, your logic and evidence. Notice, by the way, that even his theory comes in three parts. The rule of three is everywhere in rhetoric. Julius Caesar's famous message, often quoted as "veni, vidi, vici" — "I came, I saw, I conquered" — is perhaps the most famous example. Why does it work? Psychologists suggest that three is the smallest number that creates a pattern. Two items feel like a comparison; four start to feel like a list. Three feels complete. The second trick is antithesis — putting opposites side by side. Think of Neil Armstrong's line about "one small step for man" and "one giant leap for mankind". And the third is the rhetorical question, which turns a lecture into a conversation. So, what do these techniques have in common? They make your message easier to remember. And in a pitch, what matters most is what people remember after you've left the room.

## 📖 Reading — Why most pitches fail [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L4/reading.m4a)

Every year, thousands of founders stand in front of investors hoping to secure funding. Most of them fail — and, according to experienced investors, the reason is rarely the idea itself. What sinks most pitches is the way the idea is communicated. The first mistake is starting in the wrong place. Many founders open with their company history or a detailed description of their technology. Investors, however, want to understand the problem before they care about the solution. A strong pitch begins with a pain point the audience can feel — ideally one that is large, urgent and growing. The second mistake is trying to say too much. Faced with a short time slot, nervous presenters often try to cram every feature onto their slides. The result is confusion. What investors remember is a single clear message: who the product is for, what it does, and why it is better than the alternatives. The third mistake is neglecting evidence. Enthusiasm is necessary, but it is not sufficient. Investors want to see traction — paying customers, growth rates, partnerships — that proves the idea works outside the founder's imagination. Finally, many pitches simply end. The speaker reaches the last slide, says "thank you" and waits for questions. A compelling pitch, by contrast, finishes with a clear call to action: what the founder is asking for and what the investor will gain. The best founders understand that a pitch is not a lecture. It is the beginning of a relationship. And, as with any relationship, first impressions matter enormously.

**Questions:**
1. What sinks most pitches, according to investors?
2. Where should a strong pitch begin?
3. What is traction? Give examples.
4. How should a compelling pitch end?

## 📝 Writing

Write the script for a 3-minute pitch (300–350 words) for a product or social project. Include at least one rhetorical question, one rule of three, one antithesis, two cleft sentences and clear signposting.

**Model:**

> Pitch script: AulaViva  
>   
> How many of you had a teacher who changed your life? (Pause.) Now imagine if that teacher, after retiring, could keep changing lives — from home, a few hours a week.  
>   
> That's the idea behind AulaViva.  
>   
> Let me start with the problem. Across Brazil, many students who struggle at school have no access to extra help. Private tutoring is expensive, and public schools are often overloaded. At the same time, thousands of experienced teachers retire every year. They have the knowledge, the patience and, very often, the desire to keep teaching. What's missing is a bridge between these two groups.  
>   
> AulaViva is that bridge. We're an online platform that connects retired teachers with students who need support. Sessions are affordable for families, flexible for teachers and free for students from low-income households, thanks to sponsorship from local businesses.  
>   
> This brings me to our results. We launched a pilot in two cities last year. In six months, 120 retired teachers joined the platform and delivered more than 3,000 hours of tutoring. Participating schools reported that most students improved their maths grades. But it was the teachers who surprised us most: many told us they felt useful and connected again.  
>   
> So, what makes us different? It's not just a tutoring app; it's a community. The reason why our teachers stay is that they feel valued, not just paid.  
>   
> Now, let's look at the opportunity. Brazil has millions of students who could benefit, and the number of retired teachers is growing every year. We're ready to expand to ten more cities.  
>   
> To wrap up, we're looking for an investment of two million reais to fund this expansion and develop our mobile app. In return, we're offering a 15% stake in a company with proven impact, a scalable model and a mission that matters.  
>   
> What we're asking for today isn't charity. It's a partnership. Thank you.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — Pitch à americana

O estilo de pitch do Vale do Silício é **direto, confiante e otimista** — às vezes até exagerado para padrões britânicos ou brasileiros. No Reino Unido, um tom mais **modesto** e algum humor autodepreciativo costumam funcionar melhor. Conheça seu público: o que soa "confiante" em São Francisco pode soar "arrogante" em Londres.
