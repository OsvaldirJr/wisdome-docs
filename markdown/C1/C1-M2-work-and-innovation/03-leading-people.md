"Ele **pode muito bem** ser promovido." "Já que estamos aqui, **é melhor** começarmos." "Isso **deve** ficar pronto até sexta." Os modais no C1 servem para **graduar certeza** e mostrar **atitude** com precisão — habilidade essencial para quem lidera pessoas.

## 🧩 Grammar: Advanced modals: probability and attitude

**1. Graduando a probabilidade (do mais certo ao menos certo):**
- **will / be bound to / be sure to / be certain to** → certeza: "There**'s bound to** be resistance." (com certeza)
- **should / ought to** → expectativa lógica: "The report **should be** ready by Friday." (é o esperado)
- **may well / might well / could well** → **bem provável** (o "well" aumenta a chance): "She **might well** get the job."
- **may / might / could** → possível
- **might just / could just** → pequena chance (às vezes com esperança): "It **might just** work!"
- **be unlikely to / I doubt** → improvável

**2. May / might as well** → "é melhor / tanto faz, então..." (sugestão sem entusiasmo, porque não há opção melhor):
- "The meeting is cancelled. We **may as well** go home."
- "Nobody's listening. I **might as well** be talking to a wall." (= é como se...)

**3. Could hardly / can't possibly** → quase impossível:
- "I **could hardly** say no to my boss." / "We **can't possibly** finish by Monday."

**4. Would para expectativa e crítica:**
- "You**'d think** a manager **would** know that." (seria de esperar)
- "She **would** say that, wouldn't she?" (típico dela)
- "I**'d have thought** so." (eu acharia que sim)

**5. No passado:** might well have, is bound to have, should have (expectativa: "They **should have arrived** by now.")

| Modal | Grau / atitude | Exemplo |
|---|---|---|
| be bound to / be sure to | certeza | He's bound to be late. |
| should | expectativa | It should take ten minutes. |
| may / might / could well | bem provável | Prices could well rise. |
| might just | pequena chance, esperança | We might just win. |
| may / might as well | sem opção melhor | We may as well wait. |
| could hardly | quase impossível | I could hardly refuse. |
| You'd think... | expectativa frustrada | You'd think he'd apologize. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L3/example-01.m4a) If you micromanage talented people, they may well start looking for another job. — _Se você microgerencia pessoas talentosas, é bem provável que elas comecem a procurar outro emprego._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L3/example-02.m4a) Any major change is bound to cause some anxiety, so communicate early and often. — _Qualquer grande mudança com certeza vai causar alguma ansiedade, então comunique cedo e com frequência._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L3/example-03.m4a) You'd think that, after three failed projects, the director would listen to the team. — _Era de se esperar que, depois de três projetos fracassados, o diretor ouvisse a equipe._

> 💡 **Dica:** Atenção: **should** de probabilidade ("It **should** be easy" = deve ser fácil) é diferente de **should** de conselho. O contexto decide. E **may well** NÃO significa "pode bem" no sentido de "tem permissão" — significa **"é bem provável"**.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ He is bound to being late.  
  ✅ **He is bound to be late.**  
  be bound to + verbo base.
- ❌ We might as well to go home.  
  ✅ **We might as well go home.**  
  might as well + verbo base (sem to).
- ❌ I could hardly not believe it.  
  ✅ **I could hardly believe it.**  
  hardly já é negativo.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Acento em may/might WELL

Em **may well / might well / could well**, o **well** é acentuado: "She might **WELL** get the job." Em **may as well**, o acento recai no verbo principal: "We may as well **GO**." E **bound to** = "báundtu".

- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L3/pron-01.m4a) She might WELL get the job.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L3/pron-02.m4a) We may as well GO home.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L3/pron-03.m4a) There's BOUND to be resistance.

<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Consultant:** A few years ago, Google ran an internal research project, known as Project Aristotle, to find out why some of its teams performed better than others. You'd think the answer would be about talent — put the smartest people together and you're bound to succeed. But that's not what they found. According to the company's own summary, the most important factor was psychological safety: whether team members feel safe to take risks, ask questions and admit mistakes without being embarrassed or punished. The concept was developed by Amy Edmondson, a professor at Harvard Business School. Her early research in hospitals produced a surprising result: the better teams appeared to report more errors — not because they made more, she concluded, but because people felt safe enough to talk about them. So, if you're a leader, the message is clear. Your team may well be hiding problems from you. And if they are, you might as well be flying blind.

## 📖 Reading — The humble leader [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M2-L3/reading.m4a)

For much of the twentieth century, the ideal leader was imagined as a confident, decisive figure who had all the answers. That image may well be changing. A growing body of research suggests that some of the most effective leaders combine ambition with a quality that is rarely associated with power: humility. In his influential study of companies that had made the leap from good to great performance, the management writer Jim Collins described what he called "Level 5 leaders" — executives who blended intense professional will with personal modesty. They were quick to give credit to others for success and to take responsibility for failure. Later studies have pointed in a similar direction, linking humble leadership with greater team engagement and learning. Why should humility matter so much? One explanation is that no leader, however talented, can possibly know everything in a complex organization. Leaders who admit what they don't know are more likely to ask for input, and their teams are more likely to offer it. Arrogant leaders, by contrast, are bound to hear only what they want to hear. None of this means that leaders should be passive or indecisive. Teams still need direction, and there will be moments when someone simply has to decide. But the old idea that admitting a mistake is a sign of weakness looks increasingly outdated. You'd think, after decades of evidence, that this lesson would be widely accepted. In many workplaces, however, it is still waiting to be learned.

**Questions:**
1. What was the traditional image of an ideal leader?
2. How did Collins describe "Level 5 leaders"?
3. Why does humility matter, according to the text?
4. Does the text say leaders should be passive?

## 📝 Writing

Write an email (250–300 words) from a new team leader to their team, introducing themselves, explaining their leadership style and preparing the team for upcoming changes. Use at least six advanced modals.

**Model:**

> Subject: Hello from your new team leader  
>   
> Dear all,  
>   
> As most of you will know by now, I'm joining the customer experience team as your new leader from Monday. I wanted to take this opportunity to introduce myself and share a few thoughts about how I like to work.  
>   
> I've spent the last eight years in customer service, first as an agent and later as a supervisor, so I have a pretty good idea of what your days look like — the good, the bad and the frustrating. That said, every team is different, and I'm bound to have a lot to learn about how things work here. Over the next few weeks, I'd like to meet each of you individually. The meetings should take about thirty minutes, and Ana will send out a schedule shortly.  
>   
> In terms of style, I try to be hands-on without micromanaging. I'll set clear goals, but I trust you to decide how to reach them. I'd much rather you came to me with a problem early than tried to hide it — mistakes are inevitable, and you'll find I'm the first to own mine.  
>   
> You may well have heard that the company is planning to introduce a new support platform later this year. I know changes like this can cause anxiety, and there may well be some difficult moments during the transition. However, I can promise that you'll be involved in the process from the start. After all, you know our customers better than anyone, so it would hardly make sense to plan this without you.  
>   
> Finally, my door — or my Teams chat — is always open. If you have questions, ideas or concerns, you might as well send them now rather than wait for our meeting!  
>   
> Looking forward to working with you all.  
>   
> Best wishes,  
> Gabriela

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — "Hierarchy" e cultura

Pesquisadores como **Erin Meyer** (*The Culture Map*) mostram que culturas variam muito na relação com hierarquia. Na Escandinávia e na Holanda, chefes são tratados como iguais e decisões costumam ser consensuais; no Brasil, no Japão e na Índia, a hierarquia tende a ser mais marcada. Em equipes internacionais, um bom líder **adapta** seu estilo.
