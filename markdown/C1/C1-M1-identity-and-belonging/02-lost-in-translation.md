Yes, I want to come", dizem "**I'd love to.**" Hoje você aprende a "economizar" palavras como um nativo, falando sobre **tradução e cultura**.

## 🧩 Grammar: Ellipsis and substitution

**Substituição** = trocar uma parte da frase por uma palavra curta. **Elipse** = simplesmente omitir o que já está claro.

**1. so / not** substituem uma oração inteira depois de think, hope, expect, suppose, believe, guess, be afraid, appear, seem:
- "Is she coming?" — "I **think so**." / "I **hope not**." / "I'm **afraid not**."
- Negativa com think/believe/expect: **I don't think so** (mais natural que "I think not", que soa formal).
- **If so / If not**: "Are you free on Friday? **If so**, let's meet."
- Formal: **do so** substitui verbo + complemento: "Applicants must register online. Those who fail to **do so** will be excluded."

**2. one / ones** substituem substantivos contáveis:
- "I don't like this shirt. Can I see the red **one**?" / "These shoes are nicer than the old **ones**."
- ⚠️ Não use one com incontáveis: ❌ "I prefer the French one wine" → "I prefer French wine."

**3. Infinitivo reduzido (to)** — repete-se só o **to**:
- "Do you want to come?" — "I'd **love to**." / "I don't **want to**." / "I **meant to**, but I forgot."

**4. Auxiliares (elipse do verbo principal):**
- "I can't speak Japanese, but my sister **can**."
- "She said she'd call, and she **did**."
- "I haven't finished yet, but I **will have** by Friday."

**5. Elipse em orações coordenadas** (muito comum na escrita):
- "Some of the participants spoke English, others (spoke) Spanish."
- "I'd like to help, but (I) can't."

| Recurso | Exemplo |
|---|---|
| I think so / I hope not | "Will it rain?" "I hope not." |
| If so / If not | Is it ready? If not, let me know. |
| do so (formal) | Those who wish to do so may leave early. |
| one / ones | The old ones were better. |
| to (infinitivo reduzido) | "Will you come?" "I'd love to." |
| auxiliar | I didn't enjoy it, but she did. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L2/example-01.m4a) "Do you think the word 'saudade' can be translated?" "Not really — at least, I don't think so." — _"Você acha que a palavra 'saudade' pode ser traduzida?" "Não muito — pelo menos, acho que não."_
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L2/example-02.m4a) Some bilingual people say they feel more confident in one language than in the other; others don't. — _Algumas pessoas bilíngues dizem que se sentem mais confiantes numa língua do que na outra; outras, não._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L2/example-03.m4a) Candidates may bring a dictionary if they wish to do so. — _Os candidatos podem trazer um dicionário, se desejarem._

> 💡 **Dica:** Brasileiros costumam responder "I think yes" (= acho que sim). O correto é **I think so**. E "I think no" → **I don't think so**.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ I think yes. / I think no.  
  ✅ **I think so. / I don't think so.**  
  so / not substituem a oração.
- ❌ "Do you want to come?" "Yes, I want."  
  ✅ **"Yes, I'd love to." / "Yes, I want to."**  
  Mantenha o to quando o verbo é omitido.
- ❌ I prefer the Italian one food.  
  ✅ **I prefer Italian food.**  
  one substitui apenas substantivos contáveis.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Ênfase no auxiliar na elipse

Quando o auxiliar fica "sozinho" no final, ele recebe **ênfase** e é pronunciado na forma **forte**: "I can't, but she **CAN** (kén)." / "She said she'd call, and she **DID**." O **to** final também é forte: "I'd love **TO** (tú)."

- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L2/pron-01.m4a) I can't, but she CAN.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L2/pron-02.m4a) She said she'd call, and she DID.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L2/pron-03.m4a) I'd love TO.

**Speaker:** Does the language you speak change the way you think? For much of the twentieth century, the strong version of this idea — that language determines thought — was largely rejected. But a weaker version has gained support. The cognitive scientist Lera Boroditsky and others have studied, for instance, an Aboriginal community in Australia, the Kuuk Thaayorre, whose language uses cardinal directions — north, south, east, west — instead of "left" and "right". Speakers have been reported to stay remarkably well oriented, even indoors. Other studies have looked at colour: Russian has separate basic words for light blue and dark blue, and some experiments found that Russian speakers were slightly faster at distinguishing those shades. Does this mean we're prisoners of our language? Most researchers would say not. But it does suggest that each language trains our attention in particular ways — and that learning another one can show us the world from a slightly different angle.

## 📖 Reading — The translator's dilemma [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L2/reading.m4a)

Every translator faces the same impossible choice: be faithful to the words or to the meaning. Translate too literally, and the result may be accurate but lifeless; translate too freely, and you risk replacing the author's voice with your own. Nowhere is this clearer than with humour, poetry and culturally loaded words. Take the Portuguese "jeitinho". A dictionary might offer "knack" or "way around something", but neither conveys the mixture of creativity, flexibility and occasional rule-bending the word suggests to Brazilians. A translator can choose to keep the original word and explain it, to find an approximate equivalent, or to rewrite the sentence entirely. Each option gains something and loses something. Machine translation has transformed the field in recent years. For everyday texts — instructions, emails, the gist of a news article — modern tools are remarkably good, and many professional translators now use them as a first draft. Literary translation, however, remains a stubbornly human craft. A novel's rhythm, ambiguity and wordplay depend on choices that require an understanding not only of two languages but of two cultures. Perhaps the best definition of a good translation is one that makes the reader forget it is one. If so, the translator's greatest achievement is to become invisible — which may explain why so few readers can name the translators of their favourite books.

**Questions:**
1. What is the translator's "impossible choice"?
2. Why is "jeitinho" hard to translate?
3. How do many professionals use machine translation?
4. Why does literary translation remain human?
