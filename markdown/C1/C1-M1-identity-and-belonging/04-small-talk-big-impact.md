Uma das diferenças mais claras entre B2 e C1 está nas **palavrinhas**: "anyway", "mind you", "as it happens", "sort of", "-ish". Elas não mudam o conteúdo, mas mudam **como você soa**: natural, relaxado e no controle da conversa. Tema: o poder do **small talk**.

- Are you good at small talk? Do you enjoy it?

## 🧩 Grammar: Spoken discourse markers and vague language

**1. Discourse markers da fala** — organizam a conversa e mostram sua atitude:
- **Mudar / voltar ao assunto:** anyway, by the way, incidentally, speaking of which, as I was saying
- **Acrescentar uma ressalva:** **mind you** ("se bem que"), **that said / having said that**, then again
- **Coincidência / informação relevante:** **as it happens**, actually, funnily enough
- **Reconsiderar:** **come to think of it**, now that I think about it, on second thoughts
- **Resumir / concluir:** so, basically, at the end of the day, all in all
- **Ganhar tempo / suavizar:** well, I mean, you know, let me see

"The hotel was a bit far from the centre. **Mind you**, the view was incredible."
"Are you going to the conference? — **As it happens**, I'm giving a talk there."

**2. Vague language** — imprecisão **intencional**, típica da fala natural (não é erro!):
- **Aproximar números/horários:** about, around, roughly, **or so**, **-ish** (sevenish, fortyish), give or take
- **Categorias:** **and stuff (like that)**, **and things**, **or something (like that)**, **and so on**
- **Suavizar:** **sort of / kind of**, a bit, slightly, quite
- **Palavras "coringa":** thing, stuff, whatsit, thingy (muito informal)

"I've been there twenty times **or so**." / "She's **sort of** shy at first." / "We talked about work **and stuff**."

⚠️ **Registro:** vague language é para a **fala informal**. Em redações e e-mails formais, seja preciso.

| Função | Exemplos |
|---|---|
| Ressalva | mind you, that said, then again |
| Coincidência | as it happens, funnily enough |
| Reconsiderar | come to think of it, on second thoughts |
| Voltar ao assunto | anyway, as I was saying |
| Números vagos | or so, -ish, roughly, give or take |
| Categorias vagas | and stuff, or something, and things like that |
| Suavizar | sort of, kind of, a bit |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L4/example-01.m4a) The new office is miles from my house. Mind you, it's right next to the metro. — _O escritório novo é longe pra caramba da minha casa. Se bem que fica do lado do metrô._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L4/example-02.m4a) Come to think of it, I've never actually asked her where she's from. — _Pensando bem, eu nunca perguntei de onde ela é._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L4/example-03.m4a) We should be there by eightish, depending on traffic and stuff. — _Devemos chegar lá pelas oito, dependendo do trânsito e tal._

> 💡 **Dica:** O equivalente ao nosso "**tipo**" é **like** ("It was, like, really weird") — muito comum entre jovens, mas excessivo soa imaturo. **Sort of / kind of** são mais neutros.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ Mind you, the hotel was expensive. (como primeira frase, sem contraste)  
  ✅ **The hotel was great. Mind you, it was expensive.**  
  Mind you adiciona uma ressalva a algo já dito.
- ❌ As it happens is a good idea.  
  ✅ **As it happens, I was thinking the same thing.**  
  As it happens introduz uma coincidência/informação relevante.
- ❌ Dear Sir, the meeting will be at nineish or something.  
  ✅ **Dear Sir, the meeting will be at 9 a.m.**  
  Vague language não cabe em textos formais.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Discourse markers e ritmo

Discourse markers costumam vir num **bloco de entonação próprio**, com uma pequena pausa: "**Mind you,** | it was cheap." / "**Anyway,** | as I was saying...". **Sort of** e **kind of** se reduzem muito: "sórta", "káinda".

- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L4/pron-01.m4a) Mind you, | it was cheap.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L4/pron-02.m4a) As it happens, | I'm free tonight.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L4/pron-03.m4a) It was sorta weird.

<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Commentator:** Small talk has a pretty bad reputation. People call it shallow, fake, a waste of time. But, as it happens, research suggests otherwise. In one well-known study, the psychologists Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn asked people to have a brief, friendly exchange with a barista instead of just ordering and leaving. Those who chatted reported a better mood and a greater sense of belonging. Sandstrom has also found that our interactions with so-called "weak ties" — the neighbour, the bus driver, the person at the gym — contribute to our happiness more than we tend to assume. Mind you, I'm not saying you should corner strangers and talk about the weather for half an hour. But a two-minute chat is sort of like social glue. It's how relationships start. Come to think of it, most of my close friends began as people I made small talk with.

## 📖 Reading — The strength of weak ties [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L4/reading.m4a)

In 1973, the sociologist Mark Granovetter published a paper with a deliberately paradoxical title: "The Strength of Weak Ties". Studying how people in a Boston suburb had found their jobs, he noticed something surprising. Many had heard about the opportunity not from close friends but from acquaintances — people they saw only occasionally. His explanation was elegant. Our close friends tend to move in the same circles we do, so they mostly know what we already know. Acquaintances, by contrast, connect us to different networks and therefore to new information. The idea has since become one of the most cited in the social sciences, and recent research has largely supported it. A large study of LinkedIn users, published in Science in 2022, found that moderately weak ties were the most useful for job mobility. That said, weak ties do more than help us find work. They also seem to matter for our well-being. A quick chat with a neighbour or a familiar face at the market can make a city feel friendlier and less anonymous. Perhaps this is why so many people felt strangely lonely during the pandemic lockdowns, even when they kept in touch with close friends online: what had disappeared were the small, casual encounters that once filled their days. Small talk, it seems, is not so small after all.

**Questions:**
1. What did Granovetter notice about how people found jobs?
2. Why are weak ties useful, according to his explanation?
3. What did the 2022 LinkedIn study find?
4. Why might people have felt lonely during lockdowns?

## 📝 Writing

Write an informal blog post (250–300 words) titled "How I learned to love small talk". Write in a natural, conversational style using at least six discourse markers and four examples of vague language.

**Model:**

> How I learned to love small talk  
>   
> I'll be honest: for most of my life, I hated small talk. Chatting about the weather, the traffic, the weekend — it all seemed sort of pointless. If I wanted to talk to someone, I wanted to talk about something real.  
>   
> That changed about three years ago, when I started working from home. At first, I loved it. No commute, no office politics, no awkward conversations by the coffee machine. Then, after six months or so, I noticed something strange. I felt lonely — and not because I missed my close friends. I spoke to them all the time. What I missed were the tiny, meaningless chats: the security guard asking about my weekend, the colleague who always complained about the air conditioning, that kind of thing.  
>   
> As it happens, I'd just read an article about "weak ties" — the acquaintances who make up a big part of our social world. Apparently, brief exchanges with them can genuinely improve our mood. So I tried an experiment. For a month, I made a point of chatting to at least one stranger a day: the woman at the bakery, the guy who walks the same dog route as me, people at the gym and so on.  
>   
> The results? Well, I didn't make any best friends. Mind you, I did start looking forward to my morning bread run. Come to think of it, I also ended up joining a running group thanks to the dog guy.  
>   
> Anyway, the point is this: small talk isn't really about the weather. It's about saying, "I see you, and I'm friendly." That said, I still draw the line at talking about the traffic for more than two minutes!

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — Small talk pelo mundo

Nos EUA, small talk com desconhecidos (no elevador, na fila) é comum e esperado. No Reino Unido, o **tempo** é o tema clássico — e reclamar dele é quase um ritual. Na Finlândia, ao contrário, o silêncio entre desconhecidos é confortável e normal. Brasileiros costumam ser calorosos e pessoais rapidamente — às vezes **pessoais demais** para padrões anglo-saxões (idade, salário, religião e vida amorosa são temas a evitar no início).
