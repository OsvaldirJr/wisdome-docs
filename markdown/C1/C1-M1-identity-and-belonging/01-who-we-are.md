- How would your closest friends describe you in three words? Would you agree?

## 🧩 Grammar: Verb forms: nuance and attitude

**1. Stative verbs no continuous** — verbos de estado (be, think, have, see, feel, love...) normalmente não vão para o continuous. Quando vão, o **sentido muda**:
- **be** → comportamento temporário: "He **is** rude." (é grosso) x "He**'s being** rude." (está sendo grosso agora)
- **think** → opinião x processo: "I **think** it's a good idea." x "I**'m thinking** about moving abroad." (considerando)
- **see** → ver x encontrar/namorar: "I **see** what you mean." x "I**'m seeing** the doctor at 3." / "She**'s seeing** someone."
- **have** → posse x atividade: "I **have** two sisters." x "We**'re having** dinner."
- **love / like** no continuous → entusiasmo informal com algo atual: "I**'m loving** this new job!"

**2. Passado e continuous para distanciamento ("tentative")** — tornam pedidos e opiniões mais suaves e educados:
- "I **was wondering** if you could..." (mais educado que "I wonder")
- "I **was hoping** we could talk." / "We **were thinking** of leaving early."
- "**Did** you **want** a coffee?" (oferta educada, sobre o presente)
- "I **thought** you might like this."

**3. Always / constantly / forever + continuous** → hábito irritante ou marcante:
- "He's **always interrupting** me!" / "She was **forever** losing her keys."
- Também pode ser positivo: "She's **always doing** nice things for people."

**4. Present perfect para novidades** (notícias, anúncios), seguido do past simple para os detalhes:
- "Scientists **have discovered** a new species. It **was found** in the Amazon last month."

| Forma | Efeito | Exemplo |
|---|---|---|
| be + -ing (stative) | comportamento temporário | You're being unfair. |
| think + -ing | considerar | I'm thinking of quitting. |
| I was wondering / hoping | pedido suave | I was hoping you could help. |
| always + continuous | irritação / traço marcante | He's always complaining. |
| present perfect → past | novidade + detalhes | She's won! She beat 50 people. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L1/example-01.m4a) I know I'm being a bit sensitive, but that comment really hurt. — _Sei que estou sendo um pouco sensível, mas aquele comentário me magoou._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L1/example-02.m4a) I was wondering whether you'd had a chance to look at my proposal. — _Gostaria de saber se você teve a oportunidade de olhar minha proposta._
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L1/example-03.m4a) My grandfather was forever telling stories about the town where he grew up. — _Meu avô vivia contando histórias da cidade onde cresceu._

> 💡 **Dica:** Em português, usamos o **futuro do pretérito** para suavizar ("Eu **gostaria** de saber..."). Em inglês, o mesmo efeito vem do **passado continuous**: "I **was wondering**..." — é uma das marcas mais claras de um falante C1.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ I'm knowing him for years.  
  ✅ **I've known him for years.**  
  know é stative e não muda de sentido no continuous.
- ❌ He's rude today. (comportamento temporário)  
  ✅ **He's being rude today.**  
  Comportamento temporário = be + being + adjetivo.
- ❌ I wonder if you can help me. (soa direto)  
  ✅ **I was wondering if you could help me.**  
  O passado continuous e could suavizam o pedido.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Entonação de pedidos suaves

Frases "tentativas" têm entonação **descendente-ascendente** suave no final, o que soa educado e aberto: "I was **won**dering if you could ↘↗help me...". Uma entonação plana ou só descendente pode soar exigente.

- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L1/pron-01.m4a) I was wondering if you could help me.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L1/pron-02.m4a) I was hoping we could talk.
- [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L1/pron-03.m4a) Did you want a coffee?

<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Host:** Psychologists who study what they call "narrative identity" argue that we don't just have experiences — we turn them into stories, and those stories become who we are. The researcher Dan McAdams has spent decades studying these life stories. One pattern that comes up again and again is what he calls a "redemption sequence": a story in which something bad turns into something good. "I lost my job, but it led me to find my real calling." Research in this area suggests that people who tell their lives this way tend to report higher well-being. Now, I'm not saying we should invent happy endings. But it's worth asking: what story are you telling about yourself? And is it the only possible version? Because, as it turns out, we have more freedom to rewrite it than we usually think.

## 📖 Reading — The myth of the fixed self [🔊](../../../audio/C1/C1-M1-L1/reading.m4a)

Ask people whether their personality will change much over the next ten years and most will say no. Ask them how much it has changed over the past ten years, however, and they will readily admit that it has changed considerably. Psychologists Jordi Quoidbach, Daniel Gilbert and Timothy Wilson called this the "end of history illusion": at every age, we seem to believe that the person we are today is, more or less, the finished product. Their study, published in Science in 2013, surveyed thousands of adults and found that people consistently underestimated how much their values, preferences and personalities would change in the future. The illusion has practical consequences. Because we assume our current tastes will last, we may pay too much today for things our future selves won't value — a tattoo, say, or a ticket to see a favourite band a decade from now. Longitudinal research on personality offers a more nuanced picture still. Traits such as conscientiousness and emotional stability tend to increase gradually throughout adulthood, a pattern sometimes described as "maturation". None of this means that we are endlessly changeable; there is real continuity in who we are. But it does suggest that the question "Who am I?" is better answered in pencil than in ink. The self, it seems, is less a statue than a river.

**Questions:**
1. What is the "end of history illusion"?
2. What did the 2013 study find?
3. What practical consequence of the illusion is mentioned?
4. What does the metaphor "less a statue than a river" mean?

## 📝 Writing

Write a personal essay (250–300 words) titled "The person I used to think I was". Reflect on how your sense of self has changed. Use at least two stative verbs in the continuous, one always + continuous, and one tentative past form.

**Model:**

> The person I used to think I was  
>   
> For most of my twenties, I was convinced I was an introvert. I was always making excuses to leave parties early, and I genuinely believed that I simply wasn't "a people person". It was a label I had picked up at school, where a teacher once described me as "quiet but reliable", and I wore it for years without questioning it.  
>   
> Things started to shift when I moved to Recife for work. I didn't know anyone, so I had no choice but to start conversations — with neighbours, with colleagues, with the man who sold coconut water on the corner. To my surprise, I was enjoying it. I remember calling my sister after a few months and saying, "I think I'm being someone else here." She laughed and replied that perhaps I was finally being myself.  
>   
> Looking back, I suspect I wasn't really an introvert at all. I was anxious, which is a very different thing. Being quiet had felt safer than risking rejection, and the label gave me a convenient explanation.  
>   
> None of this means I've become the life and soul of the party. I still need time alone to recharge, and there are evenings when I'd much rather read than go out. But I've stopped thinking of myself as a fixed type. These days, if someone asks me whether I'm an introvert or an extrovert, I usually answer, "It depends on the day."  
>   
> If I could talk to my younger self, I think I'd simply say: "I was wondering whether you'd ever considered that the story you tell about yourself might be wrong." Labels can be useful, but they are descriptions, not destinies.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — "Where are you from?"

Em países multiculturais como Reino Unido, EUA e Canadá, a pergunta **"Where are you from?"** pode soar delicada quando insistida ("But where are you *really* from?"), pois sugere que a pessoa não pertence ao lugar. Alternativas mais abertas: **"Where did you grow up?"** ou **"Where's home for you?"**.
