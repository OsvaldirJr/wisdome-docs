## 🧩 Grammar: Linguagem de decisão + estrutura de apresentação

**1. Apresentar opções:** "We have **three options**. **The first one is**... **Alternatively**, we could... **Another possibility would be**..."

**2. Avaliar prós e contras** (comparativos + condicionais):
- "**The main advantage of** option A **is that** it's **cheaper than** B."
- "**The drawback is that** it would take **longer**."
- "**If we chose** option B, we**'d save** time, **but** we**'d have to** hire more people." (second conditional = hipótese)
- "**It's worth considering**..." / "**There's a trade-off between** cost **and** quality."

**3. Discutir em grupo** (concordar, discordar, sugerir — revisão do curso):
- "**I see your point, but**..." / "**That's a good idea, although**..."
- "**What if we**...?" / "**How about**...?" / "**Why don't we**...?"
- "**Could we compromise**? **What if** we tried A **for three months** and then reviewed it?"

**4. Decidir:**
- "**On balance**, I think we **should go for**..."
- "**So, have we agreed that**...?" / "**Shall we go with that?**"
- "**Let's go for** option B. **We'll** review the results in June." (will = decisão)

**5. Estrutura de uma apresentação estilo TED (5 minutos):**
1. **Gancho** (pergunta, história ou dado surpreendente): "Have you ever wondered...?"
2. **Por que importa** (o problema): "Every year, ..."
3. **Sua história ou experiência** (narrativas no passado, past perfect)
4. **Sua ideia** (present simple, passiva, relative clauses)
5. **Evidências e exemplos** (reported speech: "Researchers have found that...")
6. **Imagine se...** (second conditional: "If every company did this, ...")
7. **Chamada para ação** (imperativos, will): "Tomorrow, try..."

| Etapa | Frases-chave |
|---|---|
| Apresentar opções | We have three options. / Alternatively, we could... |
| Vantagens | The main advantage is that... / It's worth... |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/B1P/B1P-M6-L4/example-01.m4a) If we chose the cheaper supplier, we'd save 20%, but the quality might suffer. — _Se escolhêssemos o fornecedor mais barato, economizaríamos 20%, mas a qualidade poderia cair._
- [🔊](../../../audio/B1P/B1P-M6-L4/example-02.m4a) There's a trade-off between speed and quality, so let's weigh up the options carefully. — _Há uma troca entre velocidade e qualidade, então vamos pesar as opções com cuidado._
- [🔊](../../../audio/B1P/B1P-M6-L4/example-03.m4a) On balance, I think we should go for option B. It's more expensive in the short term, but it's worth it in the long term. — _Pesando tudo, acho que deveríamos escolher a opção B. É mais cara a curto prazo, mas vale a pena a longo prazo._

> 💡 **Dica:** Pesquisas sobre decisão mostram que, para escolhas importantes, **escrever prós e contras** e **dormir sobre o assunto** (sleep on it) ajudam a evitar decisões impulsivas. E às vezes vale ouvir o **gut feeling** — mas explique-o com argumentos!

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ The advantage of option A is cheaper.  
  ✅ **The advantage of option A is that it's cheaper.**  
  A estrutura é: The advantage is that + frase completa.
- ❌ It's worth to try.  
  ✅ **It's worth trying.**  
  It's worth + -ing.
- ❌ If we would choose A, we would save money.  
  ✅ **If we chose A, we would save money.**  
  Second conditional: past simple depois de if.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Apresentar com impacto

Para uma apresentação marcante: **pause** antes da ideia principal, **enfatize** números e palavras-chave, **varie o ritmo** (devagar para o importante, mais rápido para contexto) e **olhe para o público**. Grave-se e ouça: você soa convencido(a) da sua ideia?

## 📖 Reading — The paradox of choice [🔊](../../../audio/B1P/B1P-M6-L4/reading.m4a)

In a supermarket in California, researchers once set up a table with samples of jam. On some days, the table offered 24 flavors; on other days, only 6. The large display attracted more visitors, but when only 6 flavors were offered, a much higher percentage of people actually bought a jar. This famous experiment, published in 2000, helped popularize an idea that psychologist Barry Schwartz called "the paradox of choice": having too many options can make it harder to decide and less satisfying once we have decided. When we have many alternatives, we worry that we might have chosen badly, and we imagine how much better the other options might have been. Later studies have shown that the effect doesn't always appear — it depends on how complex the options are and how much we know about them. Still, many experts suggest a practical strategy for everyday decisions: reduce your options to two or three, decide what really matters to you, and remember that "good enough" is often better than "perfect". As Schwartz has argued, people who look for the absolute best option are often less happy with their choices than people who are satisfied with a good one.

**Questions:**
1. What happened when 24 flavors were offered?
2. What is "the paradox of choice"?
3. Does the effect always appear?
4. What practical strategy do experts suggest?

**Model:**

> The power of asking "What do you mean?"  
>   
> Have you ever pretended to understand something just to avoid looking stupid? I'm sure you have. We all have.  
>   
> Every day, in offices, classrooms and hospitals around the world, millions of small misunderstandings happen. Some are funny. Some are very expensive.  
>   
> Five years ago, I was working at a design agency in São Paulo. One morning, a client from Canada asked us for "something clean and bold". I nodded and said I understood. I didn't. I had been studying English for years, but I was afraid to ask. Two weeks later, we presented the design. The client looked at it for a long time and then said, very politely, that it wasn't quite what he had imagined. We had to start again from zero.  
>   
> That day, I made a promise to myself: I would never pretend to understand again.  
>   
> Since then, I've learned that asking for clarification is not a weakness — it's a skill. Researchers who study communication have found that people who ask questions are often seen as more competent, not less. A question that takes ten seconds can save weeks of work.  
>   
> So here's my idea: let's make "What do you mean?" the most normal question in any meeting. Imagine if every team did this. If managers asked their teams to check understanding, and if everyone felt safe to say "I'm not sure I follow", we would have fewer mistakes, better ideas and much more trust.  
>   
> It must sound simple — and it is. But simple doesn't mean easy.  
>   
> So tomorrow, try this: in your next conversation, when you're not 100% sure, stop and ask: "What exactly do you mean?" You might feel uncomfortable for a second. But I promise you, it's worth it.  
>   
> Thank you.
