## 🧩 Grammar: Certeza, dúvida e a passiva de relato

**Expressar graus de certeza:**
- Certeza alta: "I'm **sure / certain** that..." / "It's **definitely** true." / "There's **no doubt** that..."
- Probabilidade: "It's **likely / probably** true." / "It **seems / appears that**..."
- Dúvida: "I **doubt** (that)..." / "I'm **not sure / not convinced** that..." / "It's **unlikely** that..."
- Ceticismo: "I find that **hard to believe**." / "That **sounds** too good to be true."

**Passiva de relato (reporting passive)** — muito usada em notícias quando a informação **não é confirmada** ou a fonte é geral:
- **It is said that** + frase → "It is said that the city was founded by pirates."
- **It is believed that** + frase → "It is believed that 30% of online reviews are fake."
- **It is reported / thought / estimated that**...

Outra forma: **sujeito + is said / believed + to + verbo** → "The singer **is said to be** very shy." / "The painting **is believed to be** worth millions."

**Atribuir a fonte:** **According to** + fonte ("According to the WHO...") / "**The report says / claims that**..."

💡 **Claim** = afirmar (sem prova) — sinal de cautela: "The post **claims** that coffee cures cancer."

| Grau | Expressões |
|---|---|
| Certeza | I'm sure that... / There's no doubt that... |
| Probabilidade | It's likely that... / It seems that... |
| Dúvida | I doubt that... / I'm not convinced that... |
| Ceticismo | I find that hard to believe. / It sounds too good to be true. |
| Relato sem confirmação | It is said / believed / reported that... |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/B1/B1-M5-L4/example-01.m4a) It is estimated that false news spreads faster than true news on social media. — _Estima-se que notícias falsas se espalham mais rápido que as verdadeiras nas redes sociais._
- [🔊](../../../audio/B1/B1-M5-L4/example-02.m4a) I doubt that lemon water can cure the flu. Where did you read that? — _Duvido que água com limão cure gripe. Onde você leu isso?_
- [🔊](../../../audio/B1/B1-M5-L4/example-03.m4a) According to the fact-checkers, the video is a deepfake. — _Segundo os checadores, o vídeo é um deepfake._

> 💡 **Dica:** **I doubt** é seguido diretamente pela frase afirmativa: "I doubt **it's true**" = "duvido que seja verdade" (❌ "I doubt it isn't true").

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ Is said that he is rich.  
  ✅ **It is said that he is rich. / He is said to be rich.**  
  A passiva de relato precisa do sujeito it.
- ❌ According to me, it's false.  
  ✅ **In my opinion, it's false.**  
  According to é para fontes externas, não para a sua opinião.
- ❌ The news are fake.  
  ✅ **The news is fake.**  
  News é incontável e singular.

**Journalist:** Here are five quick ways to spot fake news. One: check the source. Is it a website you know? ## 📖 Reading — Lies travel fast [🔊](../../../audio/B1/B1-M5-L4/reading.m4a)

"A lie can travel halfway around the world while the truth is putting on its shoes." This famous quote — which, ironically, is often wrongly attributed to Mark Twain — describes a real phenomenon. A well-known study by researchers at MIT analyzed more than 100,000 stories shared on Twitter between 2006 and 2017. It was found that false stories were much more likely to be retweeted than true ones and reached people significantly faster. Why? The researchers believe that fake news is usually more surprising and emotional, and people love sharing things that are new and shocking. Interestingly, the study suggested that humans, not bots, were mainly responsible for the spread. With AI tools now able to create realistic fake images and videos, experts say media literacy — the ability to evaluate information critically — is one of the most important skills of the 21st century.

**Questions:**
1. What's ironic about the quote?
2. What did the MIT study find?
3. Why does fake news spread faster?
4. What is media literacy?

## 📝 Writing

Write a short guide (120–150 words) for older family members: "How to spot fake news on WhatsApp". Use certainty/doubt language and at least one reporting passive.

**Model:**

> How to spot fake news on WhatsApp 📱  
>   
> It is estimated that millions of fake messages are shared in Brazil every day, so here are some simple tips. First, check the source: if the message says "a doctor friend said" but there's no name, I doubt it's reliable. Second, look at the date — many stories are years old. Third, be careful with messages that make you very angry or scared: they're often designed to go viral. If something seems too good (or too terrible) to be true, it probably is. Before forwarding, search for the story on a fact-checking site like Aos Fatos or Lupa. According to these sites, health and politics are the most common topics of fake news. And remember: if you're not sure, don't share!
