## 🔥 Warm-up
Por que nativos dizem "**make** a mistake" e não "do a mistake"? "**heavy** rain" e não "strong rain"? Não há regra lógica: são **collocations** — palavras que "andam juntas". Dominá-las é o que faz seu inglês soar natural. E os **idioms** dão o tempero.

- Can you think of an English expression that doesn't make sense if you translate it literally?

## 🧩 Grammar: Collocations and idioms

**1. Collocations com verbos "leves"**
- **make** (criar, produzir, resultado): make a mistake, a decision, a plan, a complaint, an effort, progress, money, friends, a difference, an excuse, a phone call
- **do** (tarefas, trabalho, atividades): do homework, the housework, research, business, a course, your best, a favour, damage, exercise
- **take** (ações, oportunidades): take a break, a risk, a photo, an exam, part in, place, advantage of, responsibility for, care of
- **have** (experiências, refeições): have a look, a chat, a good time, an argument, a shower, lunch, a go
- **pay** (dar algo): pay attention, a visit, a compliment, the price

**2. Adjetivo + substantivo** ("forte" muda em inglês!):
- **heavy** rain / traffic / smoker · **strong** coffee / wind / accent · **high** temperature / price / risk · **deep** sleep / concern · **bitter** disappointment · **close** friend · **broad** smile

**3. Advérbio + adjetivo** (intensificadores naturais):
- **highly** recommended / unlikely / successful · **deeply** grateful / concerned · **bitterly** disappointed / cold · **fully** aware · **completely** different · **perfectly** normal

**4. Idioms (expressões idiomáticas)**
- Use com moderação — idioms demais soam forçados.
- Muitos são **parecidos** com o português (break the ice = quebrar o gelo), mas outros **não**: "it's raining cats and dogs" ≈ "está chovendo canivetes"; "cost an arm and a leg" ≈ "custar os olhos da cara".
- Idioms são **fixos**: não troque palavras (❌ "a piece of pie").

**Estratégia:** ao aprender uma palavra nova, anote **com o que ela combina**, não só a tradução.

| Verbo | Collocations |
|---|---|
| make | a mistake, a decision, progress, an effort, a difference, money |
| do | homework, research, your best, a favour, business, exercise |
| take | a break, a risk, part in, place, advantage of, care of |
| have | a look, a chat, an argument, a go, fun |
| pay | attention, a visit, a compliment |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M6-L3/example-01.m4a) I've made a lot of progress this year, but I still need to pay more attention to my pronunciation. — _Progredi muito este ano, mas ainda preciso prestar mais atenção à minha pronúncia._
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M6-L3/example-02.m4a) The restaurant was highly recommended, but it cost an arm and a leg. — _O restaurante foi muito recomendado, mas custou os olhos da cara._
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M6-L3/example-03.m4a) We were bitterly disappointed when the concert was cancelled due to heavy rain. — _Ficamos profundamente decepcionados quando o show foi cancelado por causa da chuva forte._

> 💡 **Dica:** Os erros de collocation mais comuns de brasileiros: ❌ "do a mistake" (✅ make), ❌ "make a question" (✅ **ask** a question), ❌ "take a decision" (✅ **make** — embora "take" seja aceito no inglês britânico), ❌ "strong rain" (✅ heavy rain), ❌ "lose the bus" (✅ **miss** the bus).

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ I did a mistake.  
  ✅ **I made a mistake.**  
  make a mistake.
- ❌ Can I make a question?  
  ✅ **Can I ask a question?**  
  ask a question (não make).
- ❌ There was a strong rain yesterday.  
  ✅ **There was heavy rain yesterday.**  
  heavy rain (chuva forte).

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Collocations como blocos

Collocations e idioms são pronunciados como **um bloco só**, com as palavras ligadas: "make a mistake" = "mêikamistêik", "take a break" = "têikabrêik", "a piece of cake" = "apísovkêik". Aprender em blocos melhora a **fluência** e a **velocidade**.

## 📖 Reading — Chunks, not words [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M6-L3/reading.m4a)

For most of the twentieth century, language teaching focused on two things: grammar rules and individual words. But in the 1990s, the linguist Michael Lewis proposed what he called the "Lexical Approach". His central idea was simple but powerful: language is not made of grammar and words, but largely of "chunks" — groups of words that native speakers store and use as single units. Phrases like "by the way", "I'm not sure", "make a decision" or "as far as I know" are not built word by word each time we speak; they come ready-made. Corpus research — the study of very large collections of real texts — supports this view, showing that a large part of everyday language consists of recurring combinations. This helps explain why learners can know thousands of words and still sound unnatural: they combine words in ways that are grammatically correct but that native speakers simply don't use, like "do a decision" or "strong rain". It also explains why fluent speakers can talk so quickly: they don't need to build every sentence from scratch. For learners, the practical lesson is clear. When you meet a new word, don't just learn its translation. Notice the words around it, write down the whole chunk, and practise it until it comes out automatically.

## 🌎 Cultura & Curiosidade — Idioms que confundem

Cuidado com idioms que **parecem** traduções mas não são: **"to pull someone's leg"** = zoar alguém (não "puxar o pé"); **"break a leg!"** = boa sorte (no teatro); **"it's not my cup of tea"** = não é a minha praia; **"once in a blue moon"** = muito raramente. E o português **"pagar mico"** seria **"to make a fool of yourself"**.
