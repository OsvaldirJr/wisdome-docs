## 🧩 Grammar: Hedging and softening

**Hedging** é suavizar uma afirmação para soar menos absoluto, mais educado ou mais preciso. É essencial em reuniões, textos acadêmicos e conversas delicadas.

**1. Verbos que suavizam:** **seem / appear / tend / suggest / indicate**
- "The plan **seems to** have some problems." (em vez de "The plan has problems")
- "The data **suggest** that..." / "It **appears that** prices will rise."

**2. Modais de possibilidade:** **might / may / could / would**
- "That **might** not be the best approach." / "I **would** say that..." / "I **would have thought**..."

**3. Advérbios e expressões de grau:** **perhaps, possibly, arguably, presumably, to some extent, in some ways, relatively, fairly, rather, a little, somewhat**
- "It's **arguably** the most important issue." / "I **somewhat** disagree."

**4. Estruturas impessoais:** **It could be argued that... / It is often said that... / There is some evidence that...**

**5. Discordância suave** (nunca "You're wrong"):
- "**I'm not sure I agree.**" / "**I see what you mean, but**..." / "**I'm not entirely convinced.**"
- "**Couldn't it also be argued that**...?" (pergunta negativa = sugestão educada)
- "**With respect**, I see it a bit differently."

**6. Quantificadores cuidadosos:** many (not all), some, most, a significant number of — em vez de **all / everyone / never / always**.

⚠️ Não exagere: hedging demais soa inseguro. Use mais em **opiniões e temas sensíveis**, menos em **fatos claros**.

| Direto (pode soar agressivo) | Com hedging |
|---|---|
| Your idea is wrong. | I'm not entirely sure that idea would work. |
| This is the best solution. | This is arguably the best solution. |
| Politicians are corrupt. | There seems to be a serious problem with corruption in some areas. |
| You don't understand. | I think we might be seeing this from different angles. |
| Prices will rise. | Prices are likely to rise. / It appears that prices may rise. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L4/example-01.m4a) It could be argued that social media has done more harm than good, although the evidence is somewhat mixed. — _Pode-se argumentar que as redes sociais fizeram mais mal do que bem, embora as evidências sejam um tanto divergentes._
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L4/example-02.m4a) I see where you're coming from, but I'm not entirely convinced that banning phones in schools is the answer. — _Entendo seu raciocínio, mas não estou totalmente convencido de que proibir celulares nas escolas seja a resposta._
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L4/example-03.m4a) There appears to be some confusion about the budget. Perhaps we could go through the numbers again? — _Parece haver alguma confusão sobre o orçamento. Talvez pudéssemos revisar os números de novo?_

> 💡 **Dica:** Brasileiros costumam ser calorosos e diretos em opiniões entre amigos. Em ambientes internacionais, especialmente com britânicos e asiáticos, um pouco mais de hedging evita mal-entendidos — mas com americanos e holandeses, clareza também é valorizada. Ajuste ao público!

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ You are wrong about this.  
  ✅ **I'm not sure I agree. / I see it a bit differently.**  
  Afirmação direta pode soar agressiva em contexto profissional.
- ❌ All young people are addicted to phones.  
  ✅ **Many young people seem to spend a lot of time on their phones.**  
  Generalização absoluta: use quantificadores e verbos de suavização.
- ❌ It seems that is a problem.  
  ✅ **It seems that there is a problem. / It seems to be a problem.**  
  Mantenha a frase completa (there is) ou use seem to be.

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Entonação de suavização

Hedging também é **tom de voz**: fale um pouco mais devagar, com entonação **ascendente** em "I'm not sure ↗..." e pausas curtas antes do "but". Um "Well..." ou "Hmm..." no começo já suaviza a discordância.

- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L4/pron-01.m4a) Well... I'm not entirely convinced ↗.
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L4/pron-02.m4a) I see where you're coming from ↗, but...
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L4/pron-03.m4a) It could be argued that...

<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Professor:** Students often ask me why academic writing is so full of words like "may", "suggest" and "appear". Isn't it better to be confident? The answer is that good researchers try not to claim more than their evidence shows. If one study finds that coffee drinkers live longer, a careful scientist won't write "Coffee makes you live longer." They'll write something like "The results suggest that moderate coffee consumption may be associated with a lower risk of mortality." It's less exciting, but it's more honest. The study can't prove that coffee is the cause — perhaps coffee drinkers also tend to have other healthy habits. Hedging isn't weakness. It's precision. And in a world full of exaggerated headlines, I'd argue we need much more of it.

## 📖 Reading — How to disagree well [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L4/reading.m4a)

In 2008, computer scientist and essayist Paul Graham proposed a "hierarchy of disagreement" — a pyramid showing different ways people argue, from the least to the most useful. At the bottom is name-calling: attacking the person rather than the idea ("You're an idiot"). Slightly higher is responding to tone ("Why are you being so aggressive?"), which avoids the actual argument. In the middle is contradiction: simply stating the opposite view without evidence. Near the top is counterargument — giving reasons and evidence for a different position — and at the very top is refutation: identifying exactly where the other person's argument goes wrong and explaining why. Graham's model has been widely shared online, perhaps because many public debates seem to be stuck at the bottom of the pyramid. Social media, which rewards quick and emotional reactions, arguably makes things worse. Psychologists who study conflict suggest a few habits that may help: listen to understand rather than to reply, summarize the other person's view before responding ("So you're saying..."), and look for common ground. Disagreeing well doesn't mean avoiding conflict. It means making conflict productive.

**Questions:**
1. What is at the bottom of the hierarchy of disagreement?
2. What is the difference between contradiction and counterargument?
3. Why might social media make things worse?
4. Name two habits that may help.

## 📝 Writing

Write a reply (180–220 words) to a colleague's email proposing a change you partly disagree with (e.g., moving the team back to the office five days a week). Acknowledge their points, disagree diplomatically and suggest common ground.

**Model:**

> Hi Mark,  
>   
> Thank you for sharing your proposal about returning to the office five days a week. I can see you've put a lot of thought into it, and I agree with several of your points. It's true that new team members seem to learn faster when they can sit with more experienced colleagues, and some of our brainstorming sessions have arguably been less creative online.  
>   
> That said, I'm not entirely convinced that a full return is the best solution for everyone. Our productivity data from the last year appear to show that focused tasks, such as writing reports and analyzing data, have actually improved since we became hybrid. In addition, several team members have long commutes, and a sudden change might affect morale and, potentially, retention.  
>   
> Perhaps there's some common ground here. Would it be worth trying a model with three fixed office days for collaboration and training, and two flexible days for focused work? We could review the results after three months and adjust if necessary.  
>   
> I'd be happy to discuss this further in our next meeting. I really appreciate you opening the conversation.  
>   
> Best regards,  
> Luana
