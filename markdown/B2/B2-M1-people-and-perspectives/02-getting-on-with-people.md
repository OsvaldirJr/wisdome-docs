## 🔥 Warm-up
O inglês do dia a dia é cheio de **phrasal verbs** — verbo + partícula com sentido próprio. Nativos dizem "we fell out" muito mais do que "we had a disagreement". No B2, dominar os mais comuns faz você soar natural.

- Who do you get on best with in your family?
- Have you ever fallen out with a close friend? Did you make up?

## 🧩 Grammar: Phrasal verbs: tipos e posição do objeto

**1. Intransitivos (sem objeto):** fall out, make up, break up, drift apart, grow up
"We **fell out** last year, but we've **made up** now."

**2. Separáveis (transitivos)** — o objeto pode ficar **no meio ou no fim**; mas se for **pronome**, **tem que ficar no meio**:
- "She **let her friends down**." / "She **let down her friends**." ✅
- "She **let them down**." ✅ / ❌ "She let down them."
- Outros: bring up (criar), turn down (recusar), cheer up (animar), talk into (convencer), ask out (convidar para sair)

**3. Inseparáveis** — o objeto vem sempre **depois** da partícula:
- "I can always **count on her**." (❌ count her on) / "She **takes after her mother**." (puxou à mãe) / "I **ran into** an old friend." (encontrei por acaso)

**4. Três partes** (verbo + advérbio + preposição) — sempre inseparáveis:
- **put up with** (tolerar), **look up to** (admirar), **look down on** (menosprezar), **get on with** (dar-se bem), **catch up with** (colocar o papo em dia), **come up with** (ter uma ideia), **stand up for** (defender), **fall out with** (brigar com)
- "I **look up to** my grandmother." / "How do you **put up with** the noise?"

💡 **Dica de registro:** phrasal verbs são mais **informais**; em textos formais, muitas vezes existe um verbo latino equivalente: put up with → **tolerate**; fall out → **argue**; look up to → **admire**; bring up → **raise**.

| Tipo | Exemplo | Com pronome |
|---|---|---|
| Intransitivo | We fell out. | — |
| Separável | She brought up three kids. / She brought three kids up. | She brought them up. |
| Inseparável | I ran into Ana. | I ran into her. |
| Três partes | I look up to my mom. | I look up to her. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L2/example-01.m4a) My parents brought me up to stand up for what I believe in. — _Meus pais me criaram para defender aquilo em que acredito._
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L2/example-02.m4a) We used to be inseparable, but we drifted apart when she moved abroad. — _Éramos inseparáveis, mas nos afastamos quando ela se mudou para o exterior._
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L2/example-03.m4a) I ran into my ex at the supermarket — awkward! But we caught up and it was fine. — _Encontrei meu ex no supermercado por acaso — constrangedor! Mas colocamos o papo em dia e foi tranquilo._

> 💡 **Dica:** Aprenda phrasal verbs **em contexto e por tema** (relacionamentos, trabalho, viagens), nunca em listas alfabéticas. E use um bom dicionário (Cambridge, Oxford Learner's) para saber se são separáveis.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ He let down me.  
  ✅ **He let me down.**  
  Com pronome, o objeto vai no meio do phrasal verb separável.
- ❌ I can't put up this noise.  
  ✅ **I can't put up with this noise.**  
  Put up with tem três partes.
- ❌ We made up with each other after we fell out with.  
  ✅ **We made up after we fell out.**  
  Sem objeto, use as formas intransitivas (fall out, make up).

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Ênfase na partícula

Em phrasal verbs, a **partícula** costuma ser a sílaba mais forte: "We fell **OUT**", "They broke **UP**", "Don't let me **DOWN**". Em verbos de três partes, a força fica no meio: "put **UP** with", "look **UP** to".

- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L2/pron-01.m4a) We fell OUT, but we made UP.
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L2/pron-02.m4a) Don't let me DOWN.
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L2/pron-03.m4a) I look UP to her.

<summary>Transcript</summary>

**Researcher:** One of the most common things people tell us is that they've drifted apart from friends without any real conflict. Life just gets in the way: people move, have children, change jobs. Research suggests that adult friendships need regular contact to survive — not necessarily long conversations, but small gestures, like sending a message or catching up over coffee. Another key factor is how people deal with conflict. Friends who fall out but then talk openly and make up often end up closer than before. On the other hand, friendships where people keep putting up with things they don't like, without saying anything, tend to fade. So my advice is simple: reach out, and if something bothers you, bring it up — kindly.

## 📖 Reading — The friendship recession [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M1-L2/reading.m4a)

In recent years, several surveys in the United States and other countries have suggested that adults have fewer close friends than they used to. Some commentators have even started talking about a "friendship recession". The reasons are complex. People work longer hours, move more often for jobs and spend more time online. Many say they simply don't have time to catch up with friends, and that friendships slowly fade without anyone falling out. Researchers also point out that making new friends as an adult is harder than at school or university, where we meet the same people every day without trying. One frequently quoted study by Jeffrey Hall estimated that it takes around 50 hours of time together to go from acquaintance to casual friend, and more than 200 hours to become close friends. That's a lot of coffees. However, there are reasons for optimism. Many people are deliberately making time for friendship: joining running clubs, book clubs and volunteer groups, where repeated contact happens naturally. As one participant put it, "I used to wait for friendships to happen. Now I put the effort in — and I've never felt less lonely."

**Questions:**
1. What is the "friendship recession"?
2. Why is making friends harder as an adult?
3. How many hours does it take to become close friends, according to the study?
4. What are some people doing about it?

## 📝 Writing

Write an informal email (180–220 words) to a friend you haven't spoken to in a long time. Apologize for not keeping in touch, catch up on news and suggest meeting. Use at least 6 phrasal verbs.

**Model:**

> Hi Carol,  
>   
> I know it's been ages since we last spoke, and I'm really sorry I haven't been in touch. After the wedding, life just got in the way — new job, new apartment, and suddenly two years had gone by. I've thought about you so many times.  
>   
> The other day I ran into your mum at the bakery near my old house. She told me you'd been going through a really tough time around the time of my wedding. I had no idea, and I feel awful that I didn't reach out. I think I was hurt that you couldn't come, and instead of bringing it up, I just stayed quiet. That wasn't fair to either of us.  
>   
> Anyway, I'd love to catch up properly. I've got loads of news (yes, I finally gave up my terrible job at the bank!), and I want to hear everything about your life in Recife. I'm actually going to be there for a conference next month. Could we meet up for lunch?  
>   
> You've always been someone I looked up to, and I don't want us to drift apart for good. You can always count on me — I hope I can still count on you too.  
>   
> Lots of love,  
> Jade

## ✅ Resumo
- Intransitivos: fall out, make up, drift apart.

- Inseparáveis e de três partes: count on her, put up with it, look up to him.
- Registro: phrasal verb (informal) x verbo latino (formal).
