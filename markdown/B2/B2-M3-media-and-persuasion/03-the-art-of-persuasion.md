## 🧩 Grammar: Emphasis and rhetoric

**1. Cleft sentences com What** → destacam uma informação colocando-a no fim:
- Normal: "I love the design." → Ênfase: "**What I love is** the design."
- "**What we need is** more time." / "**What worries me is** the cost."
- Com ações: "**What I did was** (to) call the manager." / "**What you should do is** talk to her."
- Variações: **All I want is**... (tudo o que eu quero é) / **The thing (that) I like is**... / **The reason (why)**... / **The place where**...

**2. Cleft sentences com It** → destacam **quem / o quê / quando / onde**:
- "Ana broke the record." → "**It was Ana who** broke the record." (foi a Ana, não outra pessoa)
- "**It was in 2015 that** everything changed." / "**It's not the price that** bothers me — **it's** the quality."

**3. Do / does / did enfático** → reforça uma afirmação (muitas vezes em contraste):
- "I **do** like your idea — I just think it's expensive."
- "She **did** call you; you just didn't hear the phone."
- Em imperativos (educado e caloroso): "**Do** come in!" / "**Do** let me know."

**4. Recursos retóricos:**
- **Regra de três:** "It's faster, cheaper and greener."
- **Perguntas retóricas:** "Who doesn't want more free time?"
- **Contraste/antítese:** "It's not about working harder; it's about working smarter."
- **Repetição:** "We will try. We will fail. We will try again."
- **Inversão com Never / Not only** (formal, enfática): "**Never have I** seen such talent." / "**Not only is it** cheaper, **but it's** also faster."

| Normal | Com ênfase |
|---|---|
| I need a holiday. | What I need is a holiday. |
| The noise annoys me. | What annoys me is the noise. |
| My mother taught me to cook. | It was my mother who taught me to cook. |
| I like it. | I do like it! |
| It's cheap and fast. | Not only is it cheap, but it's also fast. |
| I've never seen anything like it. | Never have I seen anything like it. |

**Examples:**
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M3-L3/example-01.m4a) What makes this app different is that it learns from your habits. — _O que torna este app diferente é que ele aprende com seus hábitos._
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M3-L3/example-02.m4a) It wasn't the money that convinced me — it was the team. — _Não foi o dinheiro que me convenceu — foi a equipe._
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M3-L3/example-03.m4a) I do appreciate your help; I just need a bit more time to decide. — _Eu realmente agradeço sua ajuda; só preciso de um pouco mais de tempo para decidir._

> 💡 **Dica:** Cleft sentences são ótimas em **apresentações e entrevistas**: "**What I'd like to talk about today is**..." / "**The reason why** I applied for this job **is**..." / "**What I'm most proud of is**..." Elas dão tempo para pensar e criam expectativa.

## ⚠️ Atenção, brasileiro!

- ❌ What I need it's a holiday.  
  ✅ **What I need is a holiday.**  
  Não acrescente it: What + sujeito + verbo + is...
- ❌ Was my mother who taught me.  
  ✅ **It was my mother who taught me.**  
  A cleft sentence com It precisa do sujeito it.
- ❌ Never I have seen this.  
  ✅ **Never have I seen this. / I have never seen this.**  
  Com never no início, há inversão (auxiliar + sujeito).

## 🗣️ Pronunciation: Ênfase na informação destacada

Em cleft sentences, a voz faz uma pequena **pausa depois de "is"** e **acentua** a informação final: "What I **need**... is a **HOLIDAY**." Com o do enfático, o auxiliar é **forte**: "I **DO** like it!" (normalmente seria fraco).

- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M3-L3/pron-01.m4a) What I need... is a HOLIDAY.
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M3-L3/pron-02.m4a) It was ANA who broke the record.
- [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M3-L3/pron-03.m4a) I DO like it — I promise!

**Professor:** Let's look at this electric car ad. It opens with a rhetorical question: "What if your car could make the world cleaner?" That's an emotional appeal. Then we hear the rule of three: "Silent. Smart. Sustainable." Notice the alliteration too — three words starting with S. Next comes a contrast: "It's not just a car. It's a statement." ## 📖 Reading — Ethos, pathos, logos [🔊](../../../audio/B2/B2-M3-L3/reading.m4a)

More than two thousand years ago, the Greek philosopher Aristotle described three ways to persuade an audience. Remarkably, his framework is still taught in business schools, law faculties and marketing courses today. The first is ethos: credibility. We're more likely to be persuaded by someone we trust or respect — which is why advertisers use doctors, athletes and celebrities, and why speakers often begin by explaining their experience. The second is pathos: emotion. Stories, images and music can make an audience feel joy, fear, anger or hope. It is pathos that makes a charity advert with a single child's face so much more powerful than a list of statistics. The third is logos: logic. This includes facts, data, examples and clear reasoning. What effective persuaders do is combine all three. A strong pitch might begin with a personal story (pathos), present data showing the size of the problem (logos), and mention the speaker's expertise or a respected partner (ethos). Of course, the same techniques can be used to manipulate. What distinguishes persuasion from manipulation is honesty: persuasion helps people make a decision with full information, while manipulation tries to hide it. Understanding these techniques, then, is not only useful for persuading others — it's essential for protecting ourselves.

**Questions:**
1. What is ethos?
2. Why is a charity advert with a child's face so powerful?
3. How might a strong pitch combine all three?
4. What distinguishes persuasion from manipulation?

## 📝 Writing

Write a persuasive speech (200–250 words) for a cause you believe in (e.g., more bike lanes, a four-day week, reading more books). Use ethos, pathos and logos, at least 4 emphasis structures and a clear call to action.

**Model:**

> Why our city needs more bike lanes  
>   
> Who here has spent more than an hour in traffic this week? Almost everyone, I imagine. I'm a doctor, and every day I see the consequences of the way we move around this city: stress, back pain, respiratory problems.  
>   
> What many people don't realize is that the solution is surprisingly simple. Cities that have invested in safe bike lanes — like Bogotá, Copenhagen and, closer to home, Fortaleza — have seen traffic fall, air quality improve and residents become healthier. Not only are bikes cheaper than cars, but they also take up a fraction of the space.  
>   
> It was a patient of mine who changed my mind about cycling. Five years ago, she was overweight, stressed and taking medication for blood pressure. Then she started cycling to work. Today, she no longer needs the medication. What she told me last month stayed with me: "I don't feel like I'm commuting anymore. I feel free."  
>   
> I do understand the concerns. Our streets aren't safe for cyclists — yet. But that's exactly the point. What we need isn't more courage from cyclists; it's more courage from our leaders.  
>   
> So here's my request: sign the petition, come to the council meeting on Thursday, and, if you can, try cycling to work once this month. Healthier people. Cleaner air. A better city. Let's build it together.
